  • 9.1 What Is Naturalism?

    • With the decline of Logical Empiricism, most philosophers agreed that the philosophy of science had to go beyond a strict focus on logic analysis; hence came the frameworks of philosophers such as Kuhn, and sociologists such as Merton. An alternative approach to the philosophy of science that is popular today (2026) is naturalism, according to which (p. 197):

    'philosophy should be continuous with science.' ... there should be some kind of close connection between scientific theories and philosophical theories ... philosophy can use results from the sciences to help answer philosophical questions, and can do this even in the philosophy of science itself.

    This, however, raises worries about "a vicious circularity. How can we assume, at the outset, the reliability of the scientific ideas that we are trying to investigate and assess?" (p. 197-8): The idea that we have to stand outside of something to assess it, i.e. "that the philosophy of science should work from an external and more secure standpoint is often referred to as foundationalism" (p. 198). According to it, we should not rely on scientific ideas in philosophy when we have not yet established their reliability via a philosophical theory. Yet, "naturalism is opposed to foundationalism in philosophy." Naturalists tend to regard a philosophical "foundation" as neither feasible nor necessary:

    Instead, we can only hope to develop an adequate description of how knowledge and science work if we draw on scientific ideas as we go. The description of knowledge and science that results will be no more certain or secure than the scientific theories themselves.

    Beyond these common tenets, there are disagreements and differences about approaches and ideas in naturalism. Indeed, "naturalism" as a label is appealing to many, and there "is a risk that naturalism as a movement will be swamped by the overuse of the term and will dissolve into platitudes. Despite this risk, "naturalism" is the label [Godfrey-Smith uses] for much of [his] own work; this is the camp in contemporary philosophy that [he] generally [supports]".

  • 9.2 Quine and Others

    • The beginning of naturalism is associated with Quine (and his 1969 paper "Epistemology Naturalized") even though Dewey's overlooked post-1925 naturalistic work preceded Quine. Anyhow, "in 'Epistemology Naturalized' Quine made a number of claims. He first attacked the idea that philosophers should give foundations for scientific knowledge" (p. 199). As stated, there is a consensus in naturalism supporting this, yet:

    Quine also made a more radical claim. He suggested that epistemological questions ... are so closely tied to questions in scientific psychology that epistemology should not survive as a distinct field at all. Instead, epistemology should be absorbed into psychology.

    Godfrey-Smith and many others oppose this, insisting that "there is such a thing as a philosophical question, distinct from the kinds of questions asked by scientists". Indeed, many "naturalists have argued" (p. 200) that normative questions are important examples of "questions that remain relevant in naturalistic philosophy but are not directly addressed within science":

    If epistemology was absorbed by psychology, we might get a good description of how beliefs are actually formed, but apparently we would not be told which belief-forming mechanisms are good and which are bad ... The term 'normative naturalism' is sometimes used for naturalistic views that want to retain the normative side of epistemology. I should also note that although Quine's original discussions seemed to leave no place for normative questions in epistemology, toward the end of his career he modified his view, bringing it closer to normative naturalism (Quine 1990).

    Moving on, suppose we are granted some arbitrary norms; there is still the problem of applying them to the world of facts to derive value judgements. Accordingly, some naturalists hold that "value judgements relevant to epistemology should be made in an instrumental way. In philosophical discussions of decision-making, an action is said to be instrumentally rational if it is a good way of achieving the goals that the agent is pursuing, whatever those goals might be."
    Though Godfrey-Smith's writing on instrumental rationality seems a bit vague, I think he suggests here that some so-called normative naturalists draw a line right at instrumental rationality, holding that norms and goals themselves are "just a matter of personal preference" and so there is no rigorous discussion to be had about them.
    Such a deflationary version of normative naturalism seems too meagre to me, as it seems to be hardly much more than Quine's early radical naturalism, plus the admission of arbitrary "oughts" and a procedure for applying them to facts to derive judgements.
    I am inclined to agree with Godfrey-Smith in that a richer version of normative naturalism, where we can also "have meaningful debates about which goals are the right goals, as well as how to achieve a given set of goals" (p. 201) is reasonable and desirable.

    Moving on, Godfrey-Smith notes that beyond normative matters, there are further philosophical questions that may not be fully reducible to typical scientific questions. For example, "what relationship is there between the common-sense or everyday picture of human knowledge and a scientific description of how it works?" Another example "concerns relations between different sciences. Each of the particular sciences gives us fragments of a picture of what the world is like and how it runs. Do the fragments tend to fit together neatly, or are there mismatches and tensions between them?" Indeed the philosopher can criticise "particular scientific ideas ... from the point of view of an overall picture that is based in science". In any case, Godfrey-Smith rejects the notion that (p. 202):

    all work done by philosophers should be naturalistic ... My overall view of philosophy is more open-ended than this. I think philosophy benefits from its unconstrained, unpredictable character. One never knows where the next interesting idea will come from ... Even if we are especially concerned with science, the inherent adventurousness of philosophy plays a valuable role here, too. Philosophy has often served as an "incubator" for novel, speculative ideas, giving them room to develop to a point where they may become scientifically useful. When philosophy is playing this role, it is good not to be too much guided by the science of the day, because the goal is to depart from current pictures and explore new territory.

    As an example, Godfrey-Smith discusses Kant's "dynamical" view of matter "in terms of forces of attraction and repulsion" and how this view alongside further idealist philosophical work "freed up thinking about matter, and suggested the possibility of hidden connections between apparently distinct aspects of nature" (p. 203), allegedly helping pave the way for the scientific understanding of electromagnetism. So granted this capacity as an "incubator", in addition to the task of forming an overall picture of our understanding of the world, "the philosopher should embrace a role in which one tries to be partly behind science and partly before it: behind it when putting pieces of existing science together into an overall picture, and before when pushing ahead with new possibilities".

  • 9.3 The Role of Observation in Science

    • In this section, Godfrey-Smith argues that questions about empiricism and the theory-ladenness of observation can be well dealt with via a naturalistic approach, thereby demonstrating the value of the approach. Recall that arguments for the theory-ladenness of observation typically suggest that "observation cannot function as an unbiased way of testing theories (or larger units like paradigms), because observation is affected by the theoretical beliefs of the observer" (p. 204). Indeed, "[theories] might affect where you look; they might affect how you interpret what you see; and they might affect what you see."
    Regarding the first possibility, any "sensible empiricist" would agree that your beliefs affect what you look for, but this should "not affect the capacity of observation to act as a test of theory, unless scientists are refusing to look where unfriendly observations might be found".
    Regarding the second possibility, Godfrey-Smith talks about two ways theory can affect interpretations. Firstly, since scientists "use theoretical assumptions to decide which observations to take seriously", they may erroneously decide that "some apparent observations involve malfunctions or mistakes of various kinds, and can be disregarded". This relates to the "holistic nature of testing" (p. 205) and to the various background assumptions (or their failures) that one can attribute an observation to. In turn, Godfrey-Smith claims that the issue of holism does not yield a "knockdown argument against empiricist views about observation" and that empiricists can counter that "assumptions affecting the relevance of an observation to a piece of theory can themselves be tested separately" and thereby be empirically confirmed or denied.
    Secondly, theory may affect our interpretation of observations as it affects language and the meaning of terms. However, Godfrey-Smith deems that the impact can sometimes be overblown. Consider that (p. 206):

    maybe observational reports make use of theories that are so low-level that the testing of real scientific theories will never be affected. We can think of the assumption that objects generally retain their shape when we are not looking at them as "theoretical," in a sense, but the effect of this assumption on observation reports does not usually matter to testing theories in science.

    Even if "it can be shown that observation reports use language that is closely tied to the kinds of theories that are themselves being tested ... this kind of effect may or may not be a problem", for:

    not every result described in terms of the concepts preferred by theory $T$ will be an observation report that is favorable to theory $T$ ... The fact that observation reports are expressed using concepts derived from a theory need not affect the capacity of nature to say no to a theory.

    Now, we move on to the third possibility that "the experiences themselves that people have are influenced by their beliefs, including their theories". Indeed, if for a given sensation, "there are multiple possibilities, then theoretical assumptions must be used by the [sensory] system to make a choice" about what perception to generate (p. 207). The idea of erroneous perceptions can be readily exemplified by illusions, and the book gives the example of the "Müller-Lyer" optical illusion. However, for the very same example (p. 208-9):

    although it is true that the [illusion seems] to be produced by the effects of unconscious theory, some pieces of theory or background knowledge seem to have no effect on perception. Most notably, the illusion is not affected by the knowledge that it is an illusion, or by knowledge of the theory of illusions. Those pieces of background knowledge do not make the illusion go away. The mechanisms of perception seem to be influenced by some theories and not others. And the theories that do have an effect are not sophisticated scientific theories. They are low-level sets of assumptions...

    So, "our perceptual mechanisms might use low-level theories in a way that makes perception quite reliable, without these theories themselves being true". Furthermore, perception itself may be treated as (p. 208):

    a natural phenomenon, studied by fields such as psychology and psychophysics. Those disciplines tell us what perceptual mechanisms are like and what kind of connection we have to various parts of the world by means of these mechanisms. They tell us when ordinary human perception is reliable and when it is not ... Naturalistic philosophers can put all these results to use in working out how observation actually operates, and how it could operate, in science. The theory-ladenness of observation is an important philosophical problem - or, more accurately, collection of problems. It affects debates about empiricism. But the way to assess claims about theory-ladenness is to look at various bits of science itself.

    So Godfrey-Smith's conclusion seems to be that the "effects" of "theory" on perception are best understood via naturalistic appeals to the scientific study of perception itself.

  • 9.4 Science as a Process

    • This section looks at Hull's 1988 book Science as a Process. Hull takes "for granted that humans can perceive and know about the world, not just in scientific contexts but many others. He wants to understand what makes science different" (p. 209). This leads him to give a "social account of science [that] is set up with a kind of basic empiricism operating in the background ... For Hull, there is much underlying constraint coming from experiments, predictions, and the like" (p. 212), unlike (for example), in Latour's social account, as "for Latour, the empirical side of things is a resource for scientists, something they can choose to make use of in their debates; for Hull, it is a constraint".
    Still, Hull's account "is one based largely on the social organization of incentives and rewards, especially a combination of cooperation and competition ... [The] special features of science result from an interaction between the two" (p. 209).
    While Hull recognises human curiosity as a driver of science, he posits that "the main professional motivation for individual scientists is the desire for recognition" (p. 210):

    And one kind of recognition is most relevant: use. Scientists want other scientists to use their work, giving credit when they do so ... In his emphasis on credit, Hull is fairly close to the ideas of Robert Merton, as he acknowledges. Merton ... argued that recognition is the basic reward in science. There are some differences, though. Hull stresses the desire to have one's ideas used; Merton stresses being recognized as the first to come up with an idea. Often these will go together, but not always. In Hull's view, but not Merton's, if a scientist's version of an idea is used because it is especially convenient or clear, even though it was not the first, that counts as a good outcome for the scientist.

    Given the "rise of citation analysis and the influence of measures like the 'h index'" in recent times, Hull's account seems particularly compelling. Moreover, the (p. 211):

    distinctive features of science, as Hull sees them, come from this system of incentives as it operates in a special social context. Each scientist inherits the ideas and methods of his or her field from earlier workers. Individual scientists cannot do anything significant without entering a system of cooperation and trust, without using the work of others. And in order to use the work of others in ways that provide support for what you are doing, you need to give citations. So the desire to do work that is used leads to using and citing the work of others. Scientists trade credit for support, and they do so in the hope that others will do the same for them ... Hull argues that the reason fraud in science is treated as so much more serious a crime than theft ... has to do with these sorts of factors. In a case of theft or plagiarism, the only person harmed is the one stolen from. But when a case of fraud is discovered, all the scientists who used the fraudulent work will find their work on that topic deemed unreliable, and their work will not be used.

    In turn, though Godfrey-Smith remains a bit vague about this, it stands to reason that scrutinising the work you build on is important for ensuring that your own work will be correct and recognised and used, hence why Hull, unlike Popper, "argues that there is no need for individual scientists to take a cautious and skeptical attitude toward their own work; others will do this for them".
    On a different note, we may ask (p. 211-2):

    Why has the social structure that Hull describes arisen rarely, across all the different communities that have wanted to understand the world? For Hull himself, this is somewhat puzzling. He says he assumes that both curiosity and a desire for recognition are fairly basic human motivations. That is true, but curiosity is not encouraged in all cultures. In some religious contexts, curiosity is seen as dangerous or unwelcome ... The Scientific Revolution made curiosity respectable, and this was a cultural shift ... Hull's mechanism is also based on a particular kind of credit, one that involves the ongoing use of one's work. We seem to have a situation where a basic human desire for recognition and respect is shaped by the internal culture of science into a desire for something more specific ... The reward system found in science was a fairly early invention, but it did have to be devised and worked on ... When the French Academy was founded in the seventeenth century, its members initially tried to handle credit communally. This, they found, did not work, and they switched to a more individualistic approach. The Royal Society of London ... used rapid publication in the Proceedings both to allocate individual credit and to encourage people to share their ideas. [This] system, which also included anonymous refereeing of papers, is basically what has come down to us today.

  • 9.5 The Division of Scientific Labor

    • In Lakatos' and Laudan's accounts, given a scientific field, there will be (p. 213):

    competition between teams of workers developing rival theories and perhaps defending rival methods. This kind of organization ... raises questions about the relations between the goals of individual scientists and the goals of the whole field, questions that Lakatos and Laudan did not address much at all. Some naturalistic philosophy has looked closely at these relationships, using the tools of mathematical modeling.

    In particular, Kitcher (1990) looked at the issue by considering a field with two rival programmes and the problem of allocating workers between the two. Supposing there were a hypothetical "ruler of science" who could allocate resources:

    A degree of 'bet hedging' will often be advisable, even when one program is more promising than the other. A wise 'ruler of science' would often allocate most resources to the better option but some to the alternative.

    Indeed, the rationale to do so becomes especially apparent if we consider there to be "diminishing marginal returns" from adding more workers to an already crowded programme. Now granted there is no "wise ruler", we can still ask (p. 214):

    What reward system will tend to produce the same distribution of workers that a ruler from above, who only cares about the solving of the problem, would want?

    According to Kitcher, we get a reward system that gets pretty close, and which approximates what we find in much of science in practice, if we "reward only the individuals who work on the research program that succeeds, but we divide the pie equally between all the workers who chose that program". Note that here, the "pie is not cash but recognition and prestige". Also note that Kitcher's story has an "invisible hand" mechanism wherein "selfish individual behaviors [combine] to produce a good outcome for the community" (p. 215).
    More recently, others such as Strevens (2003) have modified Kitcher's account for "another reward scheme [that] is both better for the community and closer to the actual situation in science" (p. 215). In Strevens' scheme, reward among the workers in a successful programme is allocated in proportion to their contribution. This avoids "free-riding" scenarios possible in Kitcher's scheme, rewarding workers "who joined early and/or made a big difference ... more than workers who joined late and made little difference".
    Adding another dimension, Zollman (2010) notes that "scientists can often switch [programmes] if they get information suggesting that [another programme] is doing better", yet a (p. 215-6):

    good community will be one where the appearance of early success in one program does not induce too big a rush of people to join it. That appearance of early success might be misleading. Zollman shows that in some circumstances, a community can do better if information does not move around too readily - if not everyone learns too quickly about one program's appearance of early success. That can be achieved if the social network linking the workers and research programs is not too densely interconnected - if it takes a while for information to flow across the whole network. There is a delicate balance to strike here; there is no point in having people sticking with a research program indefinitely once another program is clearly solving the problem. Information has to get around eventually. A good community will preserve a diversity of approaches for a while, but not forever.

  • 9.6 More on Competition and the Goals of Science

    • This section asks some questions about the relationship between "individual success and the goals of science as a whole" (p. 216):

    a lot of work on this topic in philosophy has tended toward a rather positive view of the relationship, seeing an alignment between individual and group interests. Hull's version of this view was especially optimistic.

    However, "might science work just as well, or better, with a somewhat different reward system from what we find today? For example, do we really need the intense and egotistic competition?" (p. 216-7) In practice, some "data suggest that an aggressive faith in one's own ideas, the pushiness of a 'true believer,' can be useful in at least some fields" (p. 217). As an example:

    Skinner and Tolman were in the 'behaviorist' tradition in psychology; they wanted psychology to be experimental, quantitative, and closely focused on behavior. But Skinner's version of this approach was almost absurdly strict, While Tolman's was more flexible. Tolman was also a modest, open-minded, considerate sort of person; Skinner was dogmatic and pushy. Skinner had much more influence than Tolman. We cannot know for sure what role temperament had in explaining this difference in success, of course, but the data are suggestive ... Let's assume that pushiness and zeal work well for individuals. Does this tend to result in good outcomes for science? In this case, an argument could be made for the opposite view. I think that if Tolman had dominated mid-twentieth-century psychology rather than Skinner, it probably would have been better for the field.

    Godfrey-Smith goes on to detail how Tolman anticipated cognitive maps, yet it took the influence of Chomsky, "another ultra-confident and tenacious person, who is sure he is right" (p. 218), for the field to move past strict behaviourism. In general, it seems to Godfrey-Smith:

    that the individual benefits of scientific overconfidence and pushiness probably do not match up with benefits to science as a whole. But impressions don't count for much, and it would be good to get more data on this point, difficult though it is.

    Finally, Godfrey-Smith recalls that the incentive structures of science are changing, as nowadays "financial rewards have started to become a far more visible feature of the life of the scientist, especially in areas such as molecular biology and biotechnology", and the "race to get grants and to get papers published in high-impact journals may now lead some people to cut more corners than they would under a different regime, and perhaps more than they did in the circumstances in which Hull was writing, in the latter part of the last century".

  • My comments

    • Appealing to science in philosophy, and/or having our empirical and scientific knowledge act as constraints on philosophical ideas, arguments and explanations (where and when appropriate) seems desirable to me personally. I probably would qualify as a "naturalist", since I view science and philosophy really as a single subject and would even prefer it if science were still called "natural philosophy". Specialisation and the historical split between these fields seem to me to have created an atmosphere in philosophy where the careful and systematic study of the natural world has had a diminished role in steering and inspiring thought, and where deep questioning and more adventurous perspectives and ideas in science have sometimes been excessively discouraged and stigmatised.
