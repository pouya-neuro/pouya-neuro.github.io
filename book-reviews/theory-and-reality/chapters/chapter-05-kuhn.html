Chapter 5 — Kuhn's Revolution

  • 5.1 "The Paradigm Has Shifted"

    • Thomas Kuhn's 1962 The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, or simply Structure, is the most well-known book written about science in the last hundred years. A common view is that Structure shows that (p. 102):

    actual scientific behavior has little to do with traditional philosophical theories of rationality and knowledge. [Godfrey-Smith thinks] there is some truth in this interpretation, but it is often exaggerated. Kuhn ... [tried] to distance himself from some of the radical views of science that came after him.

    Structure even influenced common language: due to Kuhn, the word "paradigm" has a contemporary sense of "a way of seeing the world and interacting with it", which has superseded its older sense as "an illustrative example on which other cases can be modeled". To be clear, Structure does use the word in its older sense, but in his study of scientific change, Kuhn expands its meaning to denote (p. 103):
    a whole way of doing science, in some particular field. It is a package of claims about the world, methods for gathering and analyzing data, and habits of scientific thought and action.

  • 5.2 Paradigms: A Closer Look

    • This newer sense of "paradigm" referring to "a package of ideas and methods, which, when combined, make up both a view of how some part of the world works, and a way of doing science" is called its broad sense by Godfrey-Smith. Let this broad sense be the default sense that is assumed when the word "paradigm" appears by itself. Importantly:

    according to Kuhn, one part of a paradigm in the broad sense is a specific achievement, or an exemplar. This achievement might be a strikingly successful experiment, such as Mendel's experiments with peas, which eventually became the basis of modern genetics. It might be the formulation of a set of equations or laws, such as Newton's laws of motion or Maxwell's equations describing electromagnetism. Whatever it is, this achievement is a source of inspiration to others; it suggests a way to investigate the world. Kuhn often used the term "paradigm" for a specific achievement of this kind. I will call these paradigms in the narrow sense. Paradigms in the broad sense (whole ways of doing science) include within them paradigms in the narrow sense (examples that serve as models, inspiring and directing further work).

    Note that the narrow sense is essentially an application of the old sense applied in a scientific context. Moving on, when in a field, there is a prevailing paradigm, the field will be in what Kuhn calls a state of normal science, wherein working scientists (p. 104):

    tend to agree on which problems are important, on how to approach these problems, and on how to assess possible solutions. They also agree on what the world is like, at least in broad outlines. A scientific revolution occurs when one paradigm breaks down and is replaced by another.

    We can contrast Kuhn here with Popper, for whom:

    science is characterized by permanent openness, a permanent and all-encompassing critical stance, even with respect to the fundamental ideas in a field. Kuhn argued that it is false that science exhibits a permanent openness to the testing of fundamental ideas. Not only that, but science would be worse off if it had the kind of openness that philosophers have treasured.

    Furthermore:

    for Popper, all science proceeds via a single process, the process of conjecture and refutation ... "revolutions" ... just involve ... bigger conjectures and more dramatic refutations. For Kuhn, there are two distinct kinds of scientific change: change within normal science, and revolutionary science ... These two kinds of change have very different features; when we try to apply concepts such as justification, rationality, and progress ... normal and revolutionary science have to be described differently. Within normal science, there are clear and agreed-upon standards for the justification of arguments; within revolutionary science there are not. Within normal science there is clear progress; within revolutionary science it is very hard to tell. Because revolutions are essential to science, the task of describing rationality and progress in science as a whole becomes complicated.

    Moving on, in Kuhn's writing it can be difficult to tell when he is "just saying how things are and when he is making claims about what is good or bad", i.e. to tell which "parts of the theory are just descriptive, and which are normative" (p. 105). For Godfrey-Smith, the normative aspects of Kuhn are the most fascinating, e.g. his belief that "the arbitrary, personal nature of factors often influencing scientific decisions [in revolutionary periods], the rigidity of scientific indoctrination of students, the tenacity with which ideas are held by scientists and the 'conceptual boxes' that nature gets forced into [in normal science]" (p. 106) are essential, not detrimental, to the effectiveness of science.

  • 5.3 Normal Science

    • Most of this section can be best summarised via a few excerpts (p. 106-8):

    Normal science is research inspired by a striking achievement that provides a basis for further work (a paradigm in the narrow sense). Kuhn does not think that all science needs a paradigm. Each scientific field starts out in a state of 'pre-paradigm science.' During this pre-paradigm state, scientific work can go on, but it is not well organized and usually not very effective. At some point, however, some striking piece of work appears. This achievement is taken to provide insight into the workings of some part of the world, and it supplies a model for further investigation. This achievement is so impressive that a tradition of further work starts to grow up around it. The field has its first paradigm. What are some examples of paradigms? Kuhn gave examples from physics and chemistry, such as Newton's and Einstein's paradigm ... In general, Kuhn's picture has it that there is one paradigm per field per time. A paradigm's role is to organize scientific work; the paradigm coordinates the work of individuals into an efficient collective enterprise. For Kuhn, a key feature that distinguishes normal science from other kinds is the absence of debate about fundamentals. Because scientists doing normal science agree on these fundamentals, they do not waste their time arguing about the most basic issues ... Kuhn places great emphasis on the "consensus-forging" role of paradigms. He argues that without it, there is no chance for scientists to achieve a deep understanding of phenomena. Detailed work and revealing discoveries require cooperation and consensus. Cooperation and consensus require closing off debate about fundamentals.

    In the context of normal science, Kuhn regarded a degree of closed-mindedness as being useful. Though debated, Godfrey-Smith holds that Kuhn's presentation of normal science was normative, i.e. a part of his vision of what good science should look like, a vision that others such as Popper criticised.
    Godfrey-Smith then discusses Behaviourism and modern molecular genetics as examples of paradigms with their own respective basic principles, experimental tools, statistical methods, and strategies for working out appropriate experiments.
    Finally, Godfrey-Smith's discussion moves on to ask (p. 108-109):

    what is the work of a good normal scientist like? Kuhn describes much of the work done in normal science as "puzzle-solving." The normal scientist tries to use the tools and concepts provided by the paradigm to describe, model, or create new phenomena. The puzzle is trying to get a new case to fit smoothly into the framework provided by the paradigm. Kuhn used the term "puzzle" rather than "problem" for a reason. A puzzle is something we have not yet solved, but that we think does have a solution. A problem might, for all we know, have no solution. Normal science tries to apply the concepts provided by a paradigm to issues that the paradigm suggests should be soluble. Part of the guidance provided by a paradigm is guiding the selection of good puzzles. The term "puzzle" also seems to suggest that the work is in some way insignificant or trivial. Here again, Kuhn intends to convey a precise message with the term. A normal scientist does, Kuhn thinks, spend a lot of time on topics that look insignificant from the outside... But it is this close attention to detail - which only the well-organized machine of normal science makes possible - that is able to reveal deep new facts about the world.

    As we shall see, the focused pursuit of a paradigm and its puzzles turns out to be the very force that leads to the paradigm's unravelling.

  • 5.4 Anomaly and Crisis

    • Godfrey-Smith says that for Kuhn:

    a central feature of normal science ... is that the fundamental ideas associated with a paradigm are not debated ... When there is a failure to get the expected results, the good normal scientist reacts by trying to work out what mistake she or he has made.

    Alternatively, a hypothesis can be refuted, but "throwing out a paradigm is much more difficult ... [It happens] only when (1) a critical mass of anomalies has arisen and (2) a rival paradigm has appeared" (p. 109). Indeed, in 5.4, Godfrey-Smith focuses on anomalies, which are puzzles that have resisted solutions:

    Kuhn holds that all paradigms face [some] anomalies at any given time... But the anomalies tend to accumulate... Eventually, according to Kuhn, the scientists start to lose faith in their paradigm. The result is a crisis.

    Crisis science refers to the ensuing period when the existing paradigm can no longer inspire/guide scientists, and no new paradigm has yet emerged to replace it. During this period, "the most fundamental issues are back on the table for debate [and] scientists tend to suddenly become interested in philosophy, a field that [Kuhn] sees as quite useless for normal science."
    Kuhn viewed the fall of a paradigm as part of the "proper functioning" of science, and as an inevitable result of scientists applying the paradigm to puzzles until a "critical mass of anomalies" accumulates. Notably, this notion of a "critical mass" suggests that paradigms do not break down so easily at the first signs of trouble, indeed (p. 110-111):

    All paradigms constantly encounter anomalies. For a Popperian, and many forms of empiricism, these anomalies should count as "refutations" of the theory. Kuhn thinks that science does not treat these ubiquitous anomalies as refutations, and it also should not. If scientists dropped their paradigms every time a problem arose, they would never get anything done... Ideas need some protection, or they can never be properly developed.

    In Kuhn's view, science progresses by balancing between openness and resistance to empirical refutation, and this happens "implicitly [via] the social structures and transmitted traditions of scientific behavior, and [via] the quirks of the scientific mind".
    From this point on in the book, social structures become more relevant to the discussion, and so Godfrey-Smith introduces 3 "levels" of granularity to help:
    1. At the first level are individual scientists and their observations, beliefs and reasoning.
    2. At the next level are communities of scientists who collaborate and compete.
    3. At the last level lies science embedded within broader society, with bidirectional influences mediated via policy, technology, and culture.
    Kuhn's account of scientific change does not directly grapple with broader society, and as such, his account does not go above level 2. Setting aside the question of how deficient the account is due to this neglect, Godfrey-Smith poses more basic questions about Kuhn's narrative: does normal science, i.e. do scientists working within a paradigm, actually feature faith-like commitment and scientific "indoctrination"? And if such qualities are present, are they really as virtuous as Kuhn claims, essential to propelling scientific enquiry by making it organised, coordinated and efficient? Indeed, could scientists not "agree to work together in a coordinated way, not wasting time on constant discussion of fundamental issues, while retaining a cautious attitude toward their paradigm" (p. 113)?

  • 5.5 Revolutions and Their Aftermath

    • In Kuhn's account, a crisis is the very thing that is needed for scientists to become receptive to a new paradigm. A revolution may then start via "what initially appears [to be] a new paradigm in the narrow sense, an achievement that begins to inspire people and seems to point the way forward. More specifically, the new work appears to solve one or more of the problems that prompted the crisis in the old paradigm" (p. 115). As a minor note, in the Kuhnian picture and in contrast with Popperian refutation, only after a promising new candidate paradigm is found will an old paradigm be considered "falsified" (p. 114).
    This sequential process of "breakdown" via a crisis and "rebuilding" via a revolution yields "new concepts, methods and practices", and all of this is part of the proper functioning of science, just as normal science is. However, unlike under normal science, where one can (p. 114):

    easily distinguish good work from bad, rational moves from irrational ... and ... [progress] is evident as time goes by ... [if] you look at two pieces of scientific work across a revolutionary divide, it will not be clear whether there has been progress from earlier to later. It might not even be clear how to compare the theories or pieces of work at all - they may look like very different kinds of intellectual activity. The people on different sides of the divide will be 'speaking different languages.' In the climax of his book, Kuhn suggests that workers in different paradigms are living in different worlds.
    Indeed, even though "displays of problem-solving power have an important role in ... transitions between paradigms", these transitions may be better understood via analogy to gestalt switch phenomena. The analogy extends to shifts in the scientific community's standards of evidence across paradigms, as paradigms allegedly shape what and how people can observe. This is referred to as the "theory-ladenness of observation" (p. 116), and entails that observation generally cannot be regarded as a neutral guide between different paradigms. It also follows that we can't unambiguously determine if a proposed new paradigm amounts to "progress", as what evidence there is for it, and what observations can be better explained by it will in part depend on how it reshapes our observations and view of the world. Hence, there may not be clear forward paths out of scientific crises, and instead we have the "disorderly character of revolutions" (p. 115).
    So, since during crises, there won't be a clear rational basis to distinguish good ideas from bad ones, no roadmaps to follow, and since even foundational beliefs may be revised, it is not surprising that "revolutions are capricious, disorderly events. They are affected by idiosyncratic personal factors and accidents of history" (p. 115) and involve "leaps of faith".
    Moving on, Kuhn in his later work after Structure, elaborated and seemingly moderated his views a bit, holding that any scientific paradigm should respect certain basic principles; in particular, theories developed using a given paradigm (p. 115):

    should be predictively accurate, consistent with well-established theories in neighboring fields, able to unify disparate phenomena, and fruitful of new ideas and discoveries.

    Godfrey-Smith does not elaborate in 5.5 on whether the later Kuhn moderated his views on the theory-ladenness of observation, but I briefly touch on this in my comments for this chapter, and anyhow Godfrey-Smith pushes back against theory-ladenness in 9.3 via his naturalism.
    In summary, Kuhn thought that "without the tenacious commitment to a paradigm seen in normal science, investigation tends to be shallow. But without a descent into a crisis, there is no motivation to consider radically new ideas. Significant innovation requires both normal and revolutionary modes of change" (p. 117), and having both these modes is what makes science so efficient and powerful.
    Yet, assuming a critical stance, Godfrey-Smith spends the last part of section 5.5 questioning how close Kuhn's picture is to the reality of science, e.g. "was there a crisis in the state of astronomy before Copernicus, or in biology before Darwin? Was there a state of disorder following an earlier period of confident work?" Godfrey-Smith's sceptical view is that "Kuhn's emphasis on crises sometimes seems driven more by the demands of his hypothesized mechanism for scientific change than by the historical data; Kuhn's story demands crises because only a crisis can loosen the grip of a paradigm and make people receptive to alternatives" (p. 117). Godfrey-Smith goes on to consider scenarios that depart from Kuhn's narrative by doing away with his unnecessary assumption of scientific fields as being "self-contained" (isolated); in particular, he considers the un-Kuhnian possibility that "a revolution in one field might prompt a no-crisis revolution in a neighboring field" (p. 118) and presents, as an approximate example, the revolutionary work of Chomsky in linguistics as having inspired a revolution away from Behaviourism and towards a "cognitive" paradigm in psychology.

  • 5.6 Incommensurability, Relativism, and Progress

    • Kuhn held subtle, if not contradictory, views on whether our understanding of the world increases as scientific revolutions take place and science advances. One side of Kuhn would say "no" due to the incommensurability of paradigms (e.g. of an old paradigm in crisis and a new candidate paradigm), or more specifically, of meaning and of standards.
    The incommensurability of meaning refers to the supposed difficulty or inability of scientists working in different paradigms to understand each other and to communicate their ideas accurately due to differences in language, concepts and the meanings of terms. So misunderstanding or miscommunication, perhaps subtle, may prevent effective and fair comparisons. Alternatively, concepts from one paradigm may simply not have analogues, and well-posed questions in one paradigm may not be meaningful or coherent in another. So, even in principle, a certain phenomenon that seems ordinary to one paradigm, may not even be "expressible", let alone empirically assessable by another paradigm.
    Yet, Godfrey-Smith casts doubt on this form of incommensurability (p. 120):

    my impression is that historians have not found many examples of failed communication in crucial debates. Scientists are often adept at "scientific bilingualism," switching from one framework to another. And they are often able to improvise ways of bridging linguistic gaps.

    However, Kuhn also argued for the incommensurability of standards on account of different paradigms having "their own standards for what counts as a good argument or good evidence". Hence, whether a given line of reasoning and/or evidence is persuasive will depend on the paradigm we view it through. Indeed, Godfrey-Smith, taking this form of incommensurability to be more significant, goes on to discuss Kuhn's example of incommensurable standards of causal explanation (p. 120):

    Should a scientific theory be required to make causal sense of why things happen? Should we always hope to understand the mechanisms underlying events? Or can a theory be acceptable if it gives a mathematical formalism that describes phenomena without making causal sense of them?

    As a concrete case, Godfrey-Smith contrasts the non-mechanistic mathematical paradigm of the 19th century "Biometricians" on inheritance with the mechanistic gene-based paradigm of the "Mendelians" who supplanted them.
    At the end of section 5.6, Godfrey-Smith notes that Kuhn's incommensurability seemingly makes him a relativist. That is, since language and meaning, and standards for evidence, arguments and explanations change in ways such that later paradigms cannot be framed as refinements of previous ones, there isn't a solid basis for deeming that our scientific knowledge is growing and improving over time. I.e. our justified beliefs do not simply accumulate and/or become more accurate with each paradigm, or do they? Another side of Kuhn, particularly apparent in the final pages of Structure, conflicts with relativism, positing that "our present paradigms have more problem-solving power than earlier paradigms did" since "the number and precision of solutions to problems in a scientific field tend to grow over time" (p. 122-3). I will remark on this tension in my comments.

  • 5.7 The X-Rated "Chapter X"

    • In the controversial final chapter of Structure, Kuhn stresses that a paradigm shift can profoundly alter our view of what nature is like. He additionally expresses scepticism towards the conception of there being a common stable paradigm-independent universe that we inhabit, or at the very least considers such a conception as being naive, outdated and limiting (p. 124):

    Holding onto the idea of a single fixed world that science strives to describe is holding onto the last element of a conservative view of conceptual change.

    But then if reality, at least in so far as it matters, is merely constituted by our perception of it, it follows that as our view of nature changes with a paradigm shift, then so does reality itself. To be clear, it doesn't appear that Kuhn viewed such changes as happening in a causal sense given his discussion about "changes in ideas about stars, planets, and comets [leading] to astronomers 'living in different worlds'" (p. 124), as it would go against common sense to think that massive celestial objects rearrange themselves according to our opinions.
    Still, Kuhn seemingly reduces reality to what it appears to be according to our beliefs, holding that Lavoisier lived in a different world after discovering oxygen.
    Godfrey-Smith considers this part of Structure as being by far the worst, as he thinks that changing our world-view is thoroughly distinct from changing the world itself. He also notes that this part of Structure goes hand in hand with Kuhn's opposition to "the idea that the large-scale history of science involves an accumulation of more and more knowledge about how the world really works", i.e. with the incommensurability of paradigms. After all, if science is converging to something, maybe that "thing" is an accurate model of an unchanging reality. Therefore, I question whether Kuhn's incommensurability, and the views expressed in this chapter are reconcilable with his acceptance of there still being in science an "accumulation of useful results: there [being] an accumulation of problem-solving power".
    Godfrey-Smith notes that Kuhn, in his writing about these ideas, kept returning to the history of physics. However, physics may be a special case in the sciences, in that it deals "with the most low-level and fundamental entities and processes" at the limit of what we can perceive and conceive of, and so it is particularly exposed to profound change and revision. However,

    if we look at other parts of science - at chemistry and molecular biology, for example - it is much more reasonable to see a continuing growth ... in knowledge ... [e.g.] about the structures of sugars, fats, proteins, and other important molecules. There is no evidence that these kinds of results will come to be replaced, as opposed to extended, as science moves along.

    As such, some of Kuhn's pessimism may be an unwarranted result of having a fixation on physics. Godfrey-Smith concludes the section by reiterating that Kuhn does not endorse a single consistent perspective on scientific change and progress, and at other times his scepticism expresses itself against paradigms more distinctly (p. 126):

    We try to "force" nature into "boxes," but nature resists. All paradigms are doomed to fail eventually, because nature is complex and science must simplify. This view is more coherent and more interesting than Kuhn's changing worlds position.

  • 5.8 Final Thoughts about Kuhn

    • Godfrey-Smith reiterates that for Kuhn, the success of science is owed to "a complex and fragile mechanism" maintaining "a delicate balance between ... the ordered cooperation and single-mindedness of normal science, together with the ability of these behavioral patterns to break down and reconstitute themselves in revolutions." Kuhn's view quickly came to be seen as "too tightly structured, too specific" to be a "description of how science actually works" as far too much in science does not fit its mold, e.g. "paradigms need not exert the kind of psychological dominance that Kuhn describes, and large-scale changes can occur without crises".
    Still, one invaluable facet of Kuhn's work is that it popularised approaching the "philosophy of science by looking at the social structure of science and the mechanisms underlying scientific change".
    Recall that among empiricists, broad perspectives on science emphasise its commonalities with ordinary human learning and enquiry, whereas narrow views construe science as a unique enterprise. Kuhn's story places him in the latter camp (p. 127):

    science is a form of organized behavior with a specific social structure. As a consequence, science appears in this story as a rather fragile cultural achievement; subtle changes in [society] ... could result in the loss of the special mechanisms of change that [power science].

    Moving on, Godfrey-Smith notes similarities between Kuhn's story and two other mechanisms for change and/or activity. First, he notes Adam Smiths idea of the seeming "invisible hand", i.e. the emergent efficient distribution of goods across an economy that is composed of "close-minded" individuals chiefly pursuing their own selfish ends, a case where:

    "selfishness at one level leads to the general benefit ... Something similar is seen in Kuhn's theory of science: narrow-mindedness and dogmatism at the level of the individual lead to intellectual openness in science as a whole.

    However, Godfrey-Smith notes that Kuhn has been criticised on this point, i.e that his account of normal science serves to "excuse and encourage the most narrow-minded and unimaginative trends in modern science".
    Additionally, an analogy is made with Jay Gould's (and others') idea of "punctuated equilibrium" in palaeontology according to which (p. 128-9):

    organisms in evolutionary time will usually exhibit long periods of stasis, during which we see low-level tinkering but little change to fundamental structures. These periods of stasis or equilibrium are punctuated by occasional periods of much more rapid change in which new fundamental structures arise ... The rapid periods of change are disorderly and unpredictable when compared to the simplest kind of natural selection in large populations ... The analogy with Kuhn's theory of science is striking. We have the same long periods of stability and resistance to change, punctuated by unpredictable, rapid change to fundamentals.

    However, it should be noted that Gould credited Kuhn as an influence on his work, and this may somewhat "explain away" the similarity. And in any case, this highlights the impact that Kuhn has had on scientific ideation itself.

  • My comments

    • Kuhn presents us with conflicting theses: allegedly, due to the incommensurability of paradigms, there can be no such thing as the steady accumulation of scientific knowledge, but this stands in tension with Kuhn's statement that science's problem-solving power grows over time, i.e. that "the number and precision of solutions to problems in [any] scientific field [grows] over time" (p. 122-3). This state of affairs reminds me a bit of Popper rejecting any notion of confirmation while still expecting that new scientific theories should go further than refuted theories in terms of their predictive boldness and sought accuracy.
    If there is a consistently-behaved universe out there that serves as the "target" that scientific theories try to "hit" / explain, then neither Popper nor Kuhn, I think can reasonably deny the notion of progress in science. For if newer frameworks lend themselves to sharper predictions or if they can explain more or solve more problems, then the only plausible explanation for such "improvement" seems to be that they are increasing their agreement with reality.
    The later Kuhn seems to come closer to such a position, after all, he admitted that among different paradigms there will be a "shared basis for theory choice", and one such conserved criterion is that a new theory in a new paradigm should be "consistent with well-established theories in neighbouring fields" (p. 115) with different paradigms. Surely it is not too much of a stretch then to expect the new theory to be "consistent" with analogous or related theories from the old paradigm that the new paradigm has replaced? This seems not too far-fetched.
    To be clear, I think I appreciate Kuhn's point on incommensurability: different paradigms may initially seem so conceptually apart that they cannot be brought into correspondence, e.g. consider Aristotelian and Newtonian mechanics. Yet in the absence of any convincing example where we demonstrate that two paradigms of the same field cannot be compared, I tend to think that newer paradigms can generally exposit the concepts and kinds of explanations given in older paradigms, showing where previous approaches did well and where they fell short, using the language and concepts of the newer paradigm. And yes, some explanations may become more complicated, or perhaps even infeasible, but I think that this either points to the old paradigm having trivialised some relevant complex matters (in the former case) or it may be taken as an indication for there being room for a future paradigm that can come and unify and out-do both the old and current paradigms.
    One point that Kuhn was criticised for is the specificity of his account, and the apparent fact that most fields don't neatly fit into it. Indeed, I wonder if scientific revolutions entail sweeping and complete replacements of old paradigms with new ones. Perhaps a shift can be partial? E.g. in areas where there would be substantial losses to explanatory power or methodological capabilities, the old paradigm may still be retained and applied further. It then may become an important scientific question in its own right to understand why different paradigms work better in different subfields.

    Still, one major contemporary area (as of early 2026) where Kuhn's story seems to me to be eerily applicable is the field of machine learning and the "paradigm" of large feed-forward models and scaling. It is abundantly clear that a great deal of capabilities emerge from scaling training data, model size, and compute during inference, but in the frenetic pursuit of economically transformative AI, perhaps too great of an amount of capital and promise has been placed in this paradigm. Now, machine learning is a huge field, and many alternative programs have continued in parallel, but the dominance of the current paradigm and the general fixation of the community with it resembles the description of "normal science". Also, earlier work on deep neural nets, transformers and scaling can then be seen and constituting the paradigm in the narrow sense that yielded this paradigm in the broad sense.
    As for "anomalies", we may consider the limitations of the resultant models in terms of e.g. long-horizon tasks, catastrophic forgetting and continuous and online learning, reliable reasoning and factuality, training and performance efficiency, etc. However, a caveat is that these limitations have been, to an extent, anticipated, especially by those researchers with more experience in the field. Given the basic principles of how current DNNs work, e.g. with a separation between training and inference, being trained on generating plausible sequence continuations, and being limited to finite context windows (in non-recurrent architectures), almost all the "anomalies" are to some extent predictable limitations; so here we may be dealing with a case where a paradigm that has been vigorously pursued despite its foreseeable limitations, in order to see just how far it can get us in terms of performance, and so those who are misled the most may be outsiders and investors who had over-inflated expectations as they may not fully appreciate the limitations of the current technology. In any case, it is to be seen whether we will enter an era of "crisis" in machine learning, however brief, before a paradigm that can take us even further emerges.

    On a final note, Godfrey-Smith already gives some examples where the later Kuhn backed away from some of the more extreme relativist interpretations of his views, e.g. by holding that there will be some paradigm-independent criteria for good scientific theories, or by admitting that science's problem-solving power seems to grow over time. Though not elaborated upon by Godfrey-Smith, Kuhn also seems to have moderated with regard to the theory-ladenness of observation. Whereas in Structure, Kuhn pushed the idea that our observations are so deeply influenced by our paradigms that observations can't serve as a fair judge for determining which paradigms are more truthful or accurate, the later Kuhn moderated by distinguishing between stimuli, which are shared and the same (or almost the same) for all, and experience which forms from how we process the stimuli and interpret them, and which can therefore be shaped by our paradigm, i.e. our conceptual toolkit. However, even then, the later Kuhn deemed the influence of paradigms to mainly apply at the taxonomic/lexical level, i.e. more involved in shaping the abstract categories we associate with observables, rather than holding that our direct experiences of these observables are themselves warped. I think naturalism, which Godfrey-Smith later discusses, brings us essentially to a similar position: our more abstract higher-order percepts, rather than our lower-order "direct" sensations, are what are more readily shaped by our outlook on the world (e.g. paradigm). Though, I think it is safe to say that there is no hard cut-off: all along the spectrum of processing, from the most shallow to the deepest/highest levels, our scientific, and more generally, our cultural rearing may influence how we process and thereby perceive. Though importantly, the degree of malleability is presumably proportional with the level of processing; that is, the highest levels, where the most abstract associations and concepts lie, are also the most malleable, whereas simple raw features like shapes and colour are less so. This view seems aligned with the later Kuhn and leaves us with a situation where comparisons of different paradigms and theories (against each other and against observational data), though difficult, can be done.
