Chapter 8 — Science Is Political

Summary goes here.

  • 8.1 A Changing Image of Science

    • From the Enlightenment until the mid-20th century, science was generally viewed "in Western societies as a progressive, anti-authoritarian force, able to challenge and break down entrenched ideas and arrangements" (p. 173), and was regarded as central to "Enlightenment values". From the mid-20th century onwards, among progressive circles:

    Science came to be seen instead as a force in the maintenance of the status quo ... as part of a larger, multi-tentacled political structure that acts to reinforce subtle forms of exclusion and coercion, even in apparently free and democratic societies ... [and] full of hidden features that exclude some individuals while welcoming others ... The most important manifestation of this new attitude is found in some forms of feminist philosophy of science.

  • 8.2 The Man of Reason

    • A common idea in feminist thinking has been that science is "part of a structure that has perpetuated inequalities between men and women" (p. 174) keeping them "in a second-class position as thinkers, investigators, and intellectual citizens" and "society has suffered from this, and so has science itself. So reform of some kind is needed", ranging from "simple suggestions like the recruitment of more women in the sciences" to "the encouragement of a specific kind of female 'voice' in science, and, most radically, to dethroning science from its preeminent position in Western culture". Here, Godfrey-Smith draws a distinction between feminist political ideas like "equality of opportunity" and more specific philosophical ideas about science, which generally may "be categorized in terms of three overlapping projects":

    The first is feminist analysis in the history of ideas and the history of science. The second is feminist analysis of specific scientific fields and theories, especially in social science, biology, and medicine. The third is feminist epistemology, the attempt to analyze rationality, knowledge, and other basic epistemological concepts from a feminist point of view.

    Pertaining to the first and third projects, the rest of the section focuses on "Lloyd's The Man of Reason (1984). Lloyd analyzes the historical roots of ideas about knowledge and rationality - in figures such as Plato, Aristotle, Descartes, and Bacon - and also draws conclusions for epistemology" (p. 175). Allegedly (p. 175-6):

    the concept of reason evolved in Western philosophy in a way that associated reasonableness with maleness, and associated the female mind with a set of psychological traits that contrast with reasonableness ... ideas about the relationship between the mind and nature were modeled on the relationship between male and female... Femininity was associated with receptivity, intuition, empathy, and emotion... Lloyd's best example is Francis Bacon... His model for the relation between the mind and nature was the model of marriage ... A good husband is respectful, but he is also firm and definitely in charge. The scientist approaching nature should approach her with respect and restraint, but control is certainly needed: 'Nature betrays her secrets more fully when in the grip and under the pressure of art than when in enjoyment of her natural liberty.'

    Godfrey-Smith then notes that (p. 176):

    although questions of causation in these large scale historical matters are always difficult, it surely seems likely that these associations did affect both the lives of women and the path taken by science in the early modern period. The harder question is what philosophical consequences these historical facts have for us now... Evelyn Fox Keller, another prominent feminist, thinks there [still] is a real problem ... as a result of the image of science that has been established, the woman scientist has to choose between 'inauthenticity' and 'subversion' ...

    That is, she is forced to either assume or fight the "image of the scientist modeled on the patriarchal husband".

  • 8.3 Sex and Gender in Behavioral Biology

    • This section looks at (p. 177):

    a clear example of how the gender of researchers has had an effect on the development of ideas, one where science has benefited from an increasing role for women in the field. The example concerns the study of social behavior, especially sexual behavior, in non-human primates like chimps and baboons.

    The dominant picture used to be that the female sexual lives of these species are relatively passive and that (p. 177):

    there is more variation across individuals in male reproductive success than there is in females. This [being] a consequence of the fact that one male can, in principle, impregnate large numbers of females. Female reproductive success is limited in many animals by the high costs of pregnancy. This asymmetry ... was taken to support a view holding that male sexual behavior had been finely honed ... while female behavior had not, because females could do much less to affect their reproductive success.

    However, roughly coinciding "with an influx of women into the field" (p. 177-8):

    this picture began to shift in the 1970s. Careful observation revealed a more active and complex role for female primates. It became apparent that many female primates have elaborate sex lives, involving a lot more different kinds of sexual contact than one would expect based on the old picture. Females seem to engage in subtle patterns of manipulation of male behavior.

    Indeed,

    the idea that this increasing representation of women had a significant role in shifting people's views about primate behavior is widely accepted within primatology ... [perhaps because] women researchers ... tend to empathize with female primates and watched the details of their behavior more closely than their male colleagues had.

    Godfrey-Smith notes that "inexpensive genetic testing" has also played a key role in overturning such beliefs, demonstrating that "variance in [mating] success between males [can be] quite low".

  • 8.4 Feminist Epistemology

    • Godfrey-Smith reviews work "criticizing how science handles evidence and assesses theories" (p. 179) as well as work criticising "the social structure and organization of science, where that structure affects epistemological issues". This work, beyond criticism, often makes "suggestions about reform". Godfrey-Smith appeals to "categorizations used by Sandra Harding" between three kinds of feminist criticism of science (p. 179-180):

    The earliest and least controversial she calls spontaneous feminist empiricism ... [which criticises] biases and other problems in scientific work, but ... does not challenge the traditional ideals, methods, and norms of science.
    The second category is feminist empiricism. Helen Longino's work ... is probably the most influential ... [where] the aim is to revise and improve traditional ideas about science and knowledge ... in a way that remains faithful to basic empiricist ideals.
    The third category I will call radical feminist epistemology. Two main approaches might be distinguished [here, with one called] ... feminist postmodernism. This work tends to embrace relativism: members of different genders, different ethnic groups, and different socioeconomic classes see the world in fundamentally different ways, and the idea of a single "true" description of the world that transcends these different perspectives is a harmful illusion.
    The second radical approach is standpoint epistemology. This is not a relativist view; in a sense, it is more ambitious than that. Standpoint epistemology stresses the role of the "situatedness" of an investigator or knower - their physical nature, location, and status in the world. The idea is that while traditional epistemology has seen situatedness as a potential problem for an investigator, in fact it can be a strength. Standpoint theory holds that there are some facts that will be visible only from a special point of view, the point of view of people who have been oppressed or marginalized by society. Those at the margins will be able to criticize the basics ... in ways that others cannot... This is usually not a relativist position, because the marginalized are seen as having better access to crucial facts than other people have.

    Longino looked closely at objectivity: traditionally, "beliefs or investigations are said to be objective when they have the right kind of contact with real things ... [involving] an absence of bias" (p. 180-1). That is, "we avoid bias and distortion in our thinking and perceiving, and thereby come into the right kind of contact with the objects we are trying to study." However, after Kuhn, this notion came to be seen as particularly problematic. In turn, Longino's approach was "not to describe objective individuals," but to give a central role to good "communities [that] include diverse points of view, ensure minority voices are heard, and resolve disputes without coercion" (p. 181), as difficult as this may be when there are real-world pressures, there is a need for consensus, and many fringe opinions are present.
    Looking beyond communal ways to mitigate bias and distortion, more radical positions such as Standpoint theory posit that even in science, "the special vantage point occupied by women can lead to better work being done ... [as] the experiences of marginalized people have special value". However, Godfrey-Smith (and Longino) criticise this position: what added value is there exactly? Certainly, "some facts are more visible to the marginalized and oppressed, [but then] other facts will surely be more visible to the privileged" (p. 182). It would seem the best approach still would be to "pool ideas, contributed by those with different points of view ... [which] brings us back to Longino's picture" (p. 182-3).
    An alternative radical position is Feminist Postmodernism, where we "consider 'giving up altogether the endeavour to become more and more objective' and instead accept 'the existence of an irreducible plurality of alternative narratives about the way the world is'". Yet such a position undermines reform, for "reform seems to require the view that some approaches and arrangements are worse than others and could be deliberately improved." In turn, Godfrey-Smith think that people are moderating away from such views because in the end, people seek reform and improvement.

    Godfrey-Smith then considers the notion of intersectionality (p. 183):

    any individual lies in the intersection of a great many overlapping sets, and their experience cannot be predicted by looking just at the general features associated with each of the groups they fall into. This point is widely accepted, but many feminists do expect there to be some fairly definite patterning in the great soup of intellectual diversity that is due to gender differences.

    Still, Godfrey-Smith cautions, though gender clearly shapes people's lives, it isn't an easy "question whether the experience and viewpoint of women are systematically different from those of men in ways that are likely to matter to scientific disputes" (p. 184). He and many feminists are "wary about the possibility of contributing to a stereotyping of female contributions to scientific thinking ('We must have a woman on this team, Jim, so someone will pick up on the holistic, interconnected stuff ...')". Furthermore, aside from the alleged:

    differences in theoretical style, another possibility is that women will tend to bring a distinct kind of social interaction to scientific communities. feminists have sometimes suggested that women are, on the whole, less competitive and more cooperative than men, though other feminists have been very wary of generalizations of this kind ... If there really were differences of this kind, they could be expected to have quite significant consequences for scientific communities.

  • 8.5 Postmodernism and the Science Wars

    • In the 1980s, approaches from "history, sociology, and philosophy, ... cultural anthropology, classics, economics, some parts of literary theory, feminist theory, and cultural studies" (p. 185) mixed together to create the field known as "science studies" with an aim of understanding "how science developed, how it works, and what role it has". The field somewhat controversially associated itself with "the movement in the humanities known as postmodernism".
    Briefly, Godfrey-Smith introduces Modernism as "a family of ideas" including Logical Positivism aimed at shedding "unwanted clutter ... from intellectual discussion ... and [reconstructing] things from simple, basic elements ... [with] ideals of transparency and utility" (p. 185-8). In turn, "Postmodernism rejected all this" and indeed all "overarching theories and 'metanarratives' of all kinds, and the replacement of those arrogant presumptions by a mosaic of local theories, languages, and projects" (p. 186). Furthermore, Postmodernism

    [opposed] the idea that language should be analyzed as a system used to represent, or 'stand for,' objects and situations in the world ... and welcomed a 'dissolving' not just of traditional ideas of objectivity and truth, but of the idea that stable meanings are a feature of language at all.

    Indeed, a recurring narrative in Postmodernism regarding our contemporary world, is that the:

    sea of images and texts in which we live ... has undermined ordinary representational relations between symbols and objects. In understanding the role of symbols in our lives, it is no longer useful to apply concepts like accuracy, reference, and truth: behind every symbol lies not a real object, but another symbol. At other times postmodernism has become a tremendously obscure way of arguing for extreme forms of relativism.

    In any case, science studies' "friendly relations" with such trends "in the humanities affected the image that science studies came to have. And in time, there came a backlash" (p. 187). And so science studies became a battleground for the so-called "science wars":

    some of the attacks on this work came from the side of conservatism in political and social thought ... worried that transmission of the treasures and values of Western civilization was being undermined by radical leftist faculty members in universities ... via endless relativist bleating that 'science is just another approach to knowledge with no special status.'

    However, interestingly, "the most influential episode" of these critiques came from Sokal, a self-described "left-winger who felt that the Left had lost its way" as the "siren song of French philosophy and literary theory had led the Left ... away from its earlier alliance with science and landed it in a useless and pretentious quagmire" (p. 188). He wrote a nonsensical paper full of "the jargon of postmodernism to discuss progressive political possibilities implicit in recent mathematical physics" and successfully submitted it to a literary-political journal. Sokal argued this showed that "the field had lost all intellectual standards and would print anything that used the right buzzwords and expressed the appropriate political sentiments" (p. 186).
    "Many philosophers ... felt vindicated by the Sokal hoax" and some pushed for "a reassertion of Enlightenment values, the values of science, democracy, rationality, equality, and secularism" while:

    others felt that damage had been done. After years spent bridging gaps between disciplines and establishing dialogue, Sokal's work was likely to polarize everything again ... The fear was that philosophers would cease once again to pay attention to work in neighboring fields. Sociologists, on the other side, would think that the underlying conservatism of philosophy had been revealed again;

    In any case, in science studies and beyond, according to Godfrey-Smith, there "is less tolerance now for very jargon-laden and obscure writing. That is a good thing, and reason enough to be glad of what Sokal did." Furthermore, although Godfrey-Smith has focused on the period in the latter half of the 20th century when there was a shift in the progressive view of science:

    as a generally liberating force to being seen as an institution that helps maintain political inequalities ... [since] that time, attitudes have shifted again [with progressives] offering explicit defenses of science, motivated by the need to take climate change seriously ... One the other side, some pro-business conservatives, seeing anxiety about climate change as leading to excessive interference in industry, try to emphasize the uncertainties in scientific models of our effects on the climate. This landscape is not static. By the time you read this ... it might have shifted again.

  • 8.6 Values in Science

    • This section states it will look at science and values, with a focus on the question: "should science be value-free?" (p. 189). Godfrey-Smith presents the "standard way of describing the debate that ensues", with the "yes" side deeming science to be "the dispassionate and disinterested study of nature ... attempting to be guided by data without prejudices of any kind", while the "no" side deems that "it would be impossible for science to be value-free" (p. 189-190); therefore, "its best to accept ... the 'impurity' of scientific choices ... and do a better job with it. It's best to allow an integration of scientific decision-making with broader priorities and values, and do so with our eyes open."
    Godfrey-Smith thinks this version of the "discussion is being badly set up, and the options misdescribed". Indeed, science is a human endeavour and is led "by a desire to resolve questions about how the world works", i.e. due to the value we place on understanding, and so science is not "value-free". So "the ideal of an absence of values in science is really not an issue; it is all a question of what kinds of values, and what kinds of goals, are in play." In light of this, we can reframe the discussion through a distinction between "goals like understanding and truth" versus "the role of values that might be called practical, moral, or political", i.e. "between epistemic and non-epistemic values:

    Understanding the world is an epistemic value. Protecting the environment, or advancing the power of your nation over other nations, is a nonepistemic goal. And once the question is asked again - is science affected by nonepistemic values and goals, and should it be? - quite a few of people would say something very similar to what they said in the first round above. It would be impossible for science to be unaffected by the political values of scientists, their funders, and other social groups. Given that fact, it's best to accept the situation - to accept the impurity of scientific choices in this respect - and do a better job with it.

    However, Godfrey-Smith disagrees again and presents, as a key example he contests, an excerpt from Rudner's 1953 article "The Scientist qua Scientist Makes Value Judgements". Rudner argues there that "no scientific hypothesis is ever completely verified, [so] in accepting a hypothesis the scientist must make the decision that the evidence is sufficiently strong ... to warrant the acceptance of the hypothesis" (p. 192). Hence, in Rudner's picture, "evidence pushes us a certain distance, but can't take us all the way ... to accept a theory or claim", yet:
    if we have to make a further decision ... in order to 'accept' something, how do we do it? As the evidence has already taken us as far as it can, it seems that what is left is the practical side; as Rudner put it, 'How sure we need to be before we accept a hypothesis will depend on how serious a mistake would be'. The appearance of a problem here - a gap that has to be bridged, between evidence and acceptance - is a consequence of thinking about acceptance, and belief, in a particular way. It assumes an 'on-off' treatment of acceptance. You either accept a view or you don't.
    Godfrey-Smith critiques this notion of all-or-nothing acceptance, instead arguing that "if we have degrees of belief and can use them to guide action, there is no need to take the extra step that Rudner wants us to" (p. 193). In Rudner's picture, our values (e.g. how bad a mistaken assumption about the safety of a pesticide would be) are needlessly distorting our beliefs (we deem it unsafe until/unless there is overwhelming evidence otherwise), yet there is a better picture, where we first:

    work out what to believe - we work out how confident we are that various hypotheses are true [according to observational evidence]. Then we work out what to do, in light of our beliefs. Our decisions about actions make use of our degrees of belief, but not vice versa.

    This Bayesian-like view lets us "remove the appearance of a gap between evidence and acceptance of the sort that Rudner argues for" while also being practically more valuable as it allows us to consider more contingencies/scenarios and actions/outcomes. So, according to Godfrey-Smith, we can and should "keep a separation between decisions about what to believe and decisions about what to do". So in a way, the message of this section seems to be that we can go a long way towards avoiding the influence of our non-epistemic values on our scientific beliefs if we use frameworks such as "degrees of belief" that can respect the separation between beliefs and actions.

  • My comments

    • Under the "further Reading and Notes" section, Godfrey-Smith elaborates on the discussion of section 8.6, considering:

    whether moral considerations can affect the justification of beliefs in a way that does not depend on actions ... Is making inferences using racial stereotypes, for example, wrong even when practical decisions are not at stake, and even when the stereotype has some statistical value, given prevailing social circumstances? In this literature, "evidentialism" is the view that only considerations that are evidence-related should affect our beliefs (or our degrees of belief). I favor evidentialism, though this literature raises interesting questions. The position I argue for in section 8.6 is evidentialist.

    This appeal to evidentialism here appears to be a diplomatic way of rejecting any view of reality that mixes "is's" with "oughts", i.e. any viewpoints where moral or practical considerations can affect what we consider to be true (at least in an objective or natural sense).
    Since I look up to the ideal of objectivity and trying to understand the world as it is and not as we wish it were, I can sympathise with Godfrey-Smith here. I think that biasing or "weaponising" science towards any non-epistemic values only erodes society's trust in science and feeds into a relativism where there are fewer and fewer agreed-upon "truths". In the long run, the resultant lack of faith in objectivity and science may, if anything, impede morally desirable progress, as trends and data could more casually and flippantly be ignored or denied in such a world. Blanket anti-police sentiment, vaccine scepticism and climate change denialism may all be contemporary examples of this.
    In any case, unflattering or sensitive findings, e.g. supporting certain stereotypes, can always be presented and contextualised in a transparent way that highlights the limitations of the findings and warns against unwarranted or pernicious interpretations.

    Finally, for the sake of objectivity, Longino's advocacy for diverse viewpoints in science seems prudent (e.g. it does not seem ideal if there are only women or men in a certain research field). For, unlike in some other fields of work where all the relevant characteristics of good workers may seem specifiable, science, understood as the empirical and quantitative social pursuit of knowledge about reality, is such a broad, open-ended and unpredictable enterprise that having a diversity of backgrounds among scientists inherently seems like an asset
