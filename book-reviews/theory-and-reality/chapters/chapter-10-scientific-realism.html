Chapter 10 — Scientific Realism

Summary goes here.

  • 10.1 Strange Debates

    • Godfrey-Smith asks (p. 222-3):

    does science tell us (at least sometimes, when things go well) about the hidden nature of a world that exists in a mind-independent way? Scientific realists say yes. Various opponents of scientific realism say no. A 'no' answer can be reached in a number of ways. Perhaps theories can never 'reach beyond' experience in what they say. Perhaps theories can make claims that reach further, but we can't ever expect to get claims of that kind right. Or perhaps the world itself does not exist mind-independently.

    Godfrey-Smith identifies as a scientific realist yet considers the debate from both sides to often be unsatisfactory.

  • 10.2 Realism

    • Godfrey-Smith gives a definition of what he calls Common-sense Realism (p. 224):

    We all inhabit a common reality, which has a structure that exists independently of what people think and say about it, except insofar as reality is comprised of, or is causally affected by, thoughts, theories, and other symbols.

    Now, some critics admit that there is a mind-independent world, but still hold that our "knowledge of the world is limited or impossible" as our perceptions and inferences are hopelessly warped by our own minds, "so it makes no sense to think of the world of our everyday dealings as a world that exists mind independently" (p. 223). This somewhat pessimistic position may be referred to as sceptical realism.

  • 10.3 Approaching Scientific Realism

    • This section looks at issues that may arise between common-sense realism and various notions of scientific realism. Some scientific realists are open to "asserting that the world really is the way it is described by our current and best-established scientific theories" (p. 225). Others, "argue that it is a mistake to express the scientific realist position in a way that depends on the accuracy of our current scientific theories". Indeed, quite a few philosophers moderate the position and instead "say that scientific realism holds that most of our mature scientific theories are at least approximately true":

    'Mature' theories means those that have been carefully studied and survived a lot of testing. A mature theory in this sense might still be wrong, but then, [some] realists say, its success would be a 'coincidence' or a 'miracle' ... This is now called the 'no miracles' argument - scientific realism is said to be the only view that does not make the success of science in prediction into a miracle.

    Others moderate even more, presenting "scientific realism as a claim about the aims of science. There is a world we live in ... and science [merely] aims to describe it ... Science might fail ... " (p. 226). However, Godfrey-Smith thinks that appealing to "aims" is playing it too safe and "it doesn't seem to be saying very much. People can have whatever aims they want". Instead, we may dare to claim that "it's a reasonable aim of science to describe or represent the world as it really is". Godfrey-Smith comes back to defending this claim at the end of this chapter (10.6) and more broadly across chapters 12 and 13.

    Moving on, Godfrey-Smith highlights the potential for tensions between the common-sense realism view "that we inhabit a world that exists mind-independently" and the scientific realism view "that when a theory has been tested extensively, we have reason to believe what it says", by considering the scenario where "science might tell us ... whether we live in a world of that kind - a world that exists mind-independently - or not" (p. 226-7). Indeed, in certain interpretations of quantum physics, "the state of a physical system is partially determined by the act of measurement" (p. 227), and so the "the view of the world as containing mind independent objects is wrong". Even if these interpretations turn out to be false, they "show there is a possibility that science could conflict with common-sense realism".

    Another problem revolves around "the idea that scientific realism involves a sort of overall confidence in the messages of science", yet "we might have reason to have different levels of confidence, and also different kinds of confidence, in different parts of science" (p. 228). Perhaps:

    we should not think of the parts of physics that deal with the ultimate structure of reality as a model for all of science. Physics is where we deal with the most inaccessible entities, those furthest from the domain our minds are adapted to dealing with ... Those special features of physics do not apply at all in the case of molecular biology. There we deal with entities that are far from the lowest levels, entities that we have a variety of kinds of access to ... Trying to work out the right attitude to have toward biology is not the same as trying to work out the right attitude toward theoretical physics.

    Furthermore:

    In cases where theories are doing well, we might also have grounds for optimism about some features of a theory and not others ... the confidence we should have about basic physics is confidence that some structural features of the world have been captured reliably by our models and equations ... Sadi Carnot thought that heat was a fluid, but he worked out some of the basic ideas of thermodynamics accurately despite this ... some parts of science are very good at describing structure - relationships between things - without being so good at describing the objects that stand in these relationships.

  • 10.4 Challenges from Empiricism

    • This section looks at tensions between scientific (and common-sense) realism and empiricism (p. 229):

    If there was a real world existing beyond our thoughts and sensations, how could we know anything about it? Empiricists believe that our senses provide us with our only source of factual knowledge, and many have thought that sensory evidence is not good enough for us to regard ourselves as having access to a world of the kind to which the realist is committed.

    In turn, an empiricist may adopt "a position where you simultaneously say that a real world exists and also say we can never have any knowledge about it whatsoever", or they may reject realism in favour of idealism or phenomenalism, "arguing that all we could mean when we talk about 'the real world' is some sort of regularity in our experiences" (p. 230). Recall, although Logical Positivism aimed to get rid of "meaningless" metaphysical debates, in practice, it advocated for phenomenalism. Nowadays, even though many forms of empiricism have "faded, empiricist views of the meaning of scientific language stayed around, and tended to encourage the idea that all we can ultimately talk about is our own experience, so science can never hope to describe the structure of a world beyond our senses". Yet such views do not reflect scientific practice, and while some think that's fine, others such as Smart have argued that this discrepancy does matter, for if scientists really held phenomenalist convictions, it would have a noticeable impact on scientific practice (p. 230-1):

    Suppose all you really want to do is get rules for the prediction of experience, and you encounter a problem - your predictions run into trouble. Why not just make a small patch, a small change to your theory, to deal with the problem? Why should you suspect that you have gotten something seriously wrong? If you merely patch up your theory, you will probably make it more complicated and inelegant than it was before, but this does not seem a very big deal if all you want to do is predict events. So what if your predictive machine is not as neat and simple as you'd like? Smart thought that a scientist who is a realist will treat problems differently - as raising the possibility, at least, that the theory's whole picture is wrong and should be reworked.

    So, "empiricist views of scientific language, of the sort that cause problems with realism, do not fit well with how science works. But alternative views have been hard to develop. We are still waiting on a better theory of scientific language" (p. 231). The rest of this section looks at, and critiques, van Fraassen's influential empiricist antirealist view wherein:

    all we should ask of theories is that they accurately describe the observable parts of the world. Theories that do this are 'empirically adequate.' An empirically adequate theory might also describe the hidden structure of reality, but whether or not it does so, is of no interest to science. For van Fraassen, when a theory passes a lot of tests and becomes well established, the right attitude to have toward the theory is to 'accept' it, in a special sense. ... To accept a theory, for van Fraassen, is (1) to believe (provisionally) that the theory is empirically adequate, and (2) to use the concepts the theory provides when thinking about further problems and when trying to extend and refine the theory.

    Godfrey-Smith considers van Fraassen's view to be a form of instrumentalism, for it only cares about whether a theory gives the right answers, and not whether its "right answers result from some deeper match between the theory and the world". Also, Godfrey-Smith admits its possible "to aim only for empirical adequacy, in van Fraassen's sense", but thinks van Fraassen doesn't give convincing arguments for why science can't aim for more, i.e. for why science can't go beyond the "observable". Van Fraassen himself admitted that "there is no sharp divide between the observable and unobservable parts of the world", and that there is nothing inherently "unreal about the unobservable"; consider, if something is unobservable but evidence for or against its existence can be obtained via observables, then surely it can still be an object of scientific enquiry. Sure, our beliefs about structures that aren't directly observable often will not be "as reliable as our beliefs about the observable structures" (p. 233), but all this effectively means is we have to be more cautious when dealing with them. For Godfrey-Smith, there:

    is no boundary that marks the distinction between features of the world that science can reasonably aim to tell us about and features that science cannot reasonably aim to tell us about. As we learn about the world, we also learn more and more about which parts of the world we can expect to have reliable information about. And there is no reason why science should not try to describe all the aspects of the world that we can hope to gain reliable information about.

  • 10.5 Metaphysical Constructivism

    • Metaphysical Constructivism refers to views that "in some sense ... regard the world as created or constructed by scientific theorizing" (p. 234) such that "it is not possible for a scientific theory to describe the world as it exists independent of thought, because reality itself is dependent on what people say and think". Kuhn, Latour and Goodman fall under this camp, though Kuhn (unlike Latour and Goodman) was somewhat moderate in that he was willing to distinguish our paradigm-shaped view or access to the world from the actual world itself. This distinction reminds one of Kant, who distinguished between the "noumenal" world as it is and the "phenomenal" as it appears to us. Yet Godfrey-Smith questions whether "adding an extra layer, 'the phenomenal world' or something similar, between us and the real world that constrains us" (p. 235) has any practical value in helping us better understand the world. In any case, there are academics in the history, philosophy and especially in the sociology of science who are not so "moderate" and refuse to distinguish "between the construction of ideas and the construction of reality." Godfrey-Smith thinks this is due to "a determination to oppose a particular kind of view, and to emphatically signal one's opposition to it". The opposed view, which Godfrey-Smith refers to as the old "Bad View", roughly holds that "reality determines what scientists and other people think by stamping itself on the passive mind". This is deemed (p. 236):

    not only false but culturally harmful, because it suggests a passive, uncreative view of human thought. Many traditional philosophical theories are seen as implicitly committed to this Bad View. This is one source for descriptions of logical positivism as reactionary, helpful to oppressors, and so on. A result is a tendency for people to try to go as far as possible away from the Bad View. This encourages people to assert simple reversals of the Bad View's relationship between mind and world. Thus we reach the idea that theories construct reality.

    Allegedly, moderate positions are criticised while there is "little pressure within the field to discourage people from going too far in" terms of their revisionism, hence why many "embrace the idea of an 'inversion' of the traditional picture". According to Godfrey-Smith, "the result is a literature in which one error - the view that reality stamps itself on the passive mind - is exchanged for another error, the view that thought or theory constructs reality".

    Godfrey-Smith concludes the section by explicitly acknowledging his own views as a common-sense realist and scientific realist who doubts that science will overturn common-sense realism, and who advocates for caution in interpreting "the messages of the parts of science that deal with the most inaccessible and esoteric entities". Also, he says he does not "accept the no-miracles argument for scientific realism discussed in section 10.3 ... [it] is too simplistic a view of the ways in which theories can be empirically successful". Yet he does not elaborate on this point, and finishes by opining "that phenomenalism, metaphysical constructivism, and other standard views that oppose realism are false".

  • 10.6 Underdetermination and Progress

    • Scientific Realism is often viewed as a debate over "whether we should be optimistic or pessimistic about the aspirations of science to represent the world accurately" (p. 237). The so-called "pessimistic meta-induction" is that our "fundamental ideas have changed so often within science that we should always expect our current views to turn out to be wrong", and indeed there are "long lists of previously posited theoretical entities, like phlogiston and caloric, that we now think do not exist". Yet, "[optimists] reply with long lists of theoretical entities that once were questionable but which we now think definitely do exist, such as atoms, germs, and genes". Godfrey-Smith repeats his earlier suggestion that scientific realism should be appraised "in a field-by-field way" and then notes how (p. 237-8):

    discussions of optimism and pessimism about science often take place against a backdrop provided by a general view, a claim called the "underdetermination of theory by evidence." In simple terms, this is the argument that for any collection of evidence we might have, there will always be more than one theory that can, in principle, accommodate that evidence. If so, this seems to show that our preference for any particular theory must always be influenced to some extent by factors other than evidence - by simplicity, elegance, or sheer familiarity.

    Godfrey-Smith notes that there is an extreme version of the view claiming that "there can be different theories that are compatible with all possible evidence", but discussions pertaining to it seem "somewhat artificial ... involving deceptive demons or very minor differences between two theories". A more relevant version comes from Stanford (2006) and is called the argument from "recurrent transient underdetermination", and looks into the history of science, where:

    a common situation is that the evidence people had back then, which they used to make their choice of a theory, was also consistent with another theory that they had not ruled out, because they had not even thought of that theory. We can say this because we now believe the theory that no one had thought of back then. If this is how things appear when we look back, why shouldn't we expect that future scientists will look back on us the same way?

    However, Godfrey-Smith deems the argument rather harmless if it generally holds that any given "future theory is an improvement on earlier ideas, of a kind that shows the earlier ideas to have been on the right track" (p. 239). In turn, the argument seems quite "troubling if the typical case is one where new theories tend to flatly reject the main ideas in old ones". A perhaps acceptable middle-ground would be if theories "turn out to have been quite misguided about what sorts of things the world contains but nonetheless have some structural features that we can recognize as being on the right track". In any case, though he doesn't counter the argument per se, Godfrey-Smith notes that across history, scientists have always also had the option of collecting more data when faced with underdetermination in practice. The section concludes with Godfrey-Smith suggesting that there are issues "with the whole way the situation is being set up" and that he will return to this topic when looking at modern theories of evidence in chapter 12.

  • 10.7 Natural Kinds

    • Some realist positions include (p. 240):

    the view that the world is neatly divided up into 'natural kinds,' into categories (like electron, gene, and so on) that our scientific theories give labels to ... and an ideal scientific language would give us a perfect partitioning of the world - a categorization that God would agree with, if God wanted to do science. Sometimes the term 'metaphysical realism' is used for this sort of position.

    There is something right here. It would be an extremely minimal realism that just said there is a world we live in and deal with, perhaps one with no definite structure. But there are reasonable versions of realism that do not say that the world is prepackaged into neat kinds, or anything like that.

    In any case, our language provides us with groupings of objects and accordingly, the:

    term "nominalism" is sometimes used in philosophy for the idea that groupings are entirely imposed by us; any collection of things can be given a name, and the world does not compel us to categorize things in one way rather than another. An alternative view on this question is that some groupings are more natural than others - more in accordance with the way the world is.

    Godfrey-Smith goes on to note that in "many sciences, the question of whether standard terms pick out real or natural kinds is the topic of ongoing debate" (p. 241), and gives the example of schizophrenia. This example also leads to a discussion on the impact of our categorisations, which can be independent of whether or not the kinds established are truly natural. Godfrey-Smith notes that "the path through the world of a collection of objects (crop plants, for example, or an endangered species) [can be] materially affected by our forming a new classification of them". Notably, in cases where the members of a collection can be aware of their own grouping (e.g. in the case of schizophrenia), the effects of the grouping may also be mediated through members' self-conceptions. What's more, the section ends with a discussion on the topic of race, in order to highlight a case where simplistic or faulty conceptions of there being natural kinds have historically led to social groupings with real impact on those people's conditions, treatment and self-conception. However, before discussing race, the section also talks about the issue (p. 241-2):

    that there appears to be a lot of real structure in the world that is not organized into kinds. To pick a very simple example, differences in height between people are real (they do not depend on what we say and think), but tall people are not a natural kind. There is no border between the tall and the non-tall; there are just lots of gradations in height.

    This ties in with race, as "[much] thinking about human races also tends to look for definite boundaries and kinds, even when there are gradations and mixtures" (p. 243).

  • My comments

    • It seems impossible to me to justify a sincerely held radical anti-realist position. To be clear, the way I interpret scientific realism is in terms of an opposition to strong social constructivist views that deny any significance or role for the world in the formation of our (scientific) beliefs. That is, I am comfortable being metaphysically agnostic about what the world is "really like", perhaps it's all a simulation/prank, perhaps there is only "mind-stuff", in which case there could, strictly speaking, be no such thing as mind-independence, but this is not what I think radical constructivists mean when they deny nature a role. For if there isn't something like an external world that places real constraints on theories, it follows that absolutely any theory (perhaps, granted that it is internally consistent) is as viable and can do as good of a job in accounting for our perceptions as our best theories do (presumably we just need to be "open-minded" enough to see how). Yet, once you admit some notion of empirical adequacy, for example as van Fraassen does, it would seem that you accept that there is something besides our wishes and opinions that plays a role in determining the goodness or correctness of beliefs, and this to me, amounts to tacitly accepting that "an external world" plays a role in our belief formation. Furthermore, it seems to follow that our empirically validated theories must be capturing something about the structure of the world, for how else could there be agreement and corroboration, if not due to some degree of underlying similarity and coherence, even if the actual concepts and concrete contents of our best theories are at best crude metaphors.

    Thus, I claim that we have arrived at a realist position (i.e. at a position that may appropriately be termed realist), and all it took to get here were the two seemingly hard-to-deny postulates of: (1) there being such a thing as empirical adequacy, and (2) the idea that such agreement with observation has to stem from a degree of agreement in form and structure (at some level). I guess I have given something in the neighbourhood of the no-miracles argument, and since my argument and more generally the no-miracles argument can be pretty strong (if I may say so), I wonder how Godfrey-Smith interprets the no-miracles argument, and what it is that he holds against it (as he does not elaborate in the book on why he does not find the argument compelling).
