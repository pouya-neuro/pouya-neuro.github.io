Chapter 12 — Bayesianism and New Views of Evidence

Summary goes here.

  • 12.1 New Hope

    • "Through much of the twentieth century, unsolved problems about evidence hung over the philosophy of science" (p. 267). The Logical Empiricists' atomistic statement-by-statement approach to confirmation failed as it could not deal with holism in testing and meaning. Influenced by Quine, some adopted "rather vague holistic ideas about evidence", yet these were too inexplicit to explain "how a particular theory can be supported over others". Following Kuhn and the historical/sociological turn, many questioned whether observation can play a neutral evidential role at all. Godfrey-Smith suggests that if "someone had come up with a really convincing theory of how evidence works, it would have been harder to argue for radical views" of this kind. However, the "situation has now changed. Once again a large number of philosophers have real hope in a theory of confirmation and evidence. The new view is called Bayesianism".

  • 12.2 Degrees of Belief

    • Godfrey-Smith shifts from using the strong term "confirmation", to using the term "evidence": "we want to understand evidence, for and against theories in science". Furthermore, he notes that for a long time in philosophy, belief was viewed in terms of yes-or-no frameworks, with vague caveats that nothing is certain. With time, many acknowledged some notion of "degrees of belief" but did not flesh out how it could work and how to apply it. Ramsey, who saw "belief as always (or mostly) a matter of degree" (p. 269) made great progress on the topic (1926) in his short career, but his work was appreciated only later. According to Ramsey:

    It might seem that degrees of belief are very subtle features of a person - a strength of feeling, or something like that. No, said Ramsey; they are familiar and easy to get a handle on. A person's degrees of be lief are revealed in their behavior. More exactly, a person's behavior is a consequence of their degrees of belief in various hypotheses along with their preferences or goals.

    The rest of the section focuses on concrete examples, e.g. whether you would take an umbrella out on a cloudy day reflects, among some other things, your beliefs about the chances of rainfall.

  • 12.3 Understanding Evidence with Probability

    • This section introduces Bayes' Theorem and probability as a tool for working with evidence: "observational evidence can sometimes raise or lower the probability of a hypothesis" (p. 271). Given some hypothesis $h$ and observation $e$, "we can say that evidence $e$ confirms $h$ if $P(h|e) > P(h)$. That is, $e$ confirms or supports $h$ if it makes $h$ more probable than it would otherwise be" (p. 272). Godfrey-Smith states that we "can do a lot with Bayes's theorem, if it makes sense to talk about probabilities in these ways. It is common to think that it is not too hard to interpret probabilities of the form $P(e|h)$, the likelihoods. Scientific theories are supposed to tell us what we are likely to see. The probabilities that are more controversial are the prior probabilities of hypotheses, such as $P(h)$. What could this number possibly be measuring? And the posterior probability of $h$ can only be computed if we have its prior probability". Godfrey-Smith argues that historically many (p 273):

    would have liked to use Bayes's theorem to make sense of how evidence works. But it was unclear whether this use could ever be legitimate, because to use Bayes's theorem we have to make sense of the prior probabilities of theories. Many people, especially statisticians, think that only the outcomes of repeatable events, such as repeatable experiments, can be described in terms of probability. The truth of a scientific theory about the world is not at all like this. If the prior probabilities of theories do not exist or are completely unknowable, then Bayes's theorem can't help us.

  • 12.4 The Subjectivist Interpretation of Probability

    • Most attempts to analyze probability have taken probabilities to measure some real and "objective" feature of events. A probability value is seen as measuring the chance of an event happening, where this chance is somehow a feature of the event itself and its location in the world.

    However, instead of trying to pin down probabilities in the "objective world", Ramsey proposed we identify the probabilities of events with our degrees of belief. That is, we should adopt a "subjective interpretation of probability - an interpretation in which probabilities do not measure physical chances, frequencies of events, or anything like that, but measure degrees of confidence that some person has in a claim or hypothesis" (p. 274). The statistician De Finetti went even further by arguing that the "subjective" view presents the best way of interpreting probabilities. De Finetti's position is known as "subjectivism". Note that there "is no problem making sense of prior probabilities within a subjective interpretation of probability. $P(h)$, for some person at a time, is just their overall degree of confidence at that time that $h$ is true" (p. 274). The "majority of philosophers who want to use Bayes's theorem to understand evidence today make use of a subjective view of probability" (p. 275) either "because they feel they have to be in order to use Bayes's theorem" or because they "think that subjectivism is the only interpretation of probability that makes sense". Furthermore:

    Subjectivists also argue that if you have degrees of belief that are reflected in behavior, then you have to follow the rules of probability theory in how your degrees of belief in different things are related. The picture we have on the table now is one where each person at a time has a total network of degrees of belief - this is like a web of belief in Quine's sense, but with numbers attached to each belief that reflect the person's confidence. Subjectivist Bayesians argue that a person's network of degrees of belief is only 'coherent' or rational when the person follows the standard rules of the mathematics of probability.

    The standard argument is that if you don't follow the rules of probability, you are vulnerable to a "dutch book". That is, "if your degrees of belief do not conform to the principles of the probability calculus, there are possible gambling situations in which you are guaranteed to lose money, no matter how things turn out" granted "you are willing to accept any bet that fits with your degrees of belief", as it can then be shown that for every given inconsistency, there are scenarios where "you will be willing to accept combinations of bets [called a "dutch book"] that guarantee you a loss." The section then presents a simple example of a so-called "dutch book". It follows that "anyone who does not keep his degrees of belief in line with the probability calculus is irrational in an important sense" (p. 277).

  • 12.5 Bayesianism and Evidence

    • This section looks further into the promises and problems of Bayesianism. Firstly, Godfrey-Smith presents the idea that "today's posteriors are tomorrow's priors" (p. 277). He states that there are (contested) Dutch-book arguments to support updating one's beliefs according to Bayes' theorem, but does not elaborate on them. I looked a bit into this and here is the kind of argument he may have been referencing: Suppose you follow the rules of probability at present (otherwise you are susceptible to a dutch-book right off the bat) and you have well-defined beliefs for the values of $p(h)$ and $p(e|h)$ and $p(e|\neg h)$. Suppose you will be shown $e$ or $\neg e$, and then you will be shown $h$ or $\neg h$. Consider the multi-step bet, where your money will be returned to you if $\neg e$ occurs and, if $e$, the bet continues and you will be paid $p(h|e)$ if $h$ occurs and nothing if $\neg h$ occurs. According to the rules of probability, this bet is worth "$p(h|e)$", as can be shown via Bayes Rule, so you should take the bet for that price or less. Yet, if you yourself do not follow Bayes' rule during the middle step and instead assign a posterior $x$ for $h$ where $x \lt p(h|e)$, then you would be willing to sell your bet back for any price between $x$ and $ p(h|e)$, locking in a loss. The overall argument can easily be adjusted to include a small penalty for you if $\neg e$ occurs (so you don't get all your money back if the bet is cancelled, while still ensuring that the initial bet is profitable overall in expectation), thereby creating a scenario where you are willing to take a bet and then to sell it back for less (or incur a penalty if the bet is cancelled) in a way that guarantees you a loss regardless of what happens. One issue I see with this argument is about whether you would buy the bet in the first place, for if you aren't willing to apply Bayes rule during the middle step to assess the value of your position (supposing $e$ is revealed), is it reasonable to suppose that you would be willing to apply it to assess the fair price of the bet to begin with? I think a case can be made that you wouldn't take the bet to begin with. However, I suppose that similar counter-arguments can be made against any dutch-book, and really, this "counter" boils down to a blunt refutation of the premises of the dutch-book, i.e., of there being an inconsistency in beliefs that can be exploited.

    Coming back to the section, Godfrey-Smith talks about some alleged complexities, noting that "Bayesianism has to make special moves to deal with 'old evidence,' evidence known before its relation to a hypothesis is assessed. It also has a different formula to use when the evidence $e$ is itself uncertain - this is important, because most philosophers accept that you can never be entirely certain about what you have seen". The first point seems a bit vague, but I think it essentially is about the issues you face when you aim to introduce a new $h$ or assign a posterior $p(h|e)$ if you haven't already had a prior $p(h)$ before becoming aware of $e$; if you are not careful, your knowledge of $e$ will bear on your decision about $p(h)$, and it might not then be appropriate to consider a posterior of the form $p(h|e)$, for this could lead to a sort of "double-weighting" of the evidence $e$. The second point Godfrey-Smith makes about factoring in our uncertainty about $e$ seems very fair but also relatively tractable.

    In any case, Godfrey-Smith thinks that we should not view Bayesianism as a theory for deriving "objective" probabilities. That is, we should never say something like: "'given our evidence, the probability that Darwinism is true is $0.9$.' Instead, we should say something like 'A rational person who starts out here [with priors ... ] ... and sees this evidence ... will end up with a degree of belief in Darwinism of 0.9" (p. 278). Indeed, the dependence of beliefs on freely chosen priors is often taken as a limitation of the theory as "Bayesianism cannot criticize very strange initial assignments of probability". In response, Bayesians have often argued that our starting priors (good or bad) will get "washed out" with enough evidence. There are convergence arguments that can be made to show this, but Godfrey-Smith notes that the same mathematics also can show that for any finite amount of data, there will always be priors that will yield arbitrarily dissimilar posteriors. Another deeper issue with these washing-out arguments has to do with likelihoods: the convergence of people's priors depends on them having the same likelihoods (p. 280):

    This agreement might be present, but there is no general reason why it should be. (This is another aspect of the problem of holism.) Presentations of Bayesianism sometimes use examples that involve familiar gambling games or sampling processes, in which it seems that there will surely be agreement about likelihoods even when people have different priors. But those cases are not typical of science.

    Moving on, another important question is about whether there is "any reason to think that managing our degrees of belief and our evidence in this [Bayesian] way will help us get to the truth". There has been some relatively recent work (Joyce, 1998) on this matter. Suppose you assign some events to values of either 1 or 0 (depending on whether the events do or do not obtain), and then you compute a "truthfulness" score in terms of the deviation of your probabilistic beliefs about the occurrence of these events from their assigned discrete values. It turns out that "if you want the best score ... one thing you should do is follow the rules of probability theory". Bayesianism can also deal with some of the problems for confirmation noted earlier in the book like "Hempel's ravens problem and Goodman's 'grue' problem" (p. 281). In particular, a Bayesian approach to resolving the "green" vs "grue" induction could be that:

    the "all emeralds are green" hypothesis will, for most people, have a much higher prior probability than the "all emeralds are grue" hypothesis. Then although both hypotheses are confirmed by the observations, the green-emerald hypothesis ends up with a much higher posterior probability than the grue-emerald hypothesis. That is the difference between the two inductions ... A person's [low] prior probability for the grue emerald hypothesis will usually be the result of much past experience with colors, minerals, and so on.

    I think an argument can be made that this framing just kicks the can further down the road about what (if anything) makes "green" more "probable" or "natural" and thereby deserving of a prior. Next, Godfrey-Smith recalls the Hypothetico-Deductive (HD) view of confirmation or evidence, which is "the idea that, roughly speaking, theories are confirmed when their observational consequences are seen to be true" (p. 281). He notes that Bayesianism can accommodate the HD-view nicely, as we may identify the logical consequences of a theory with observations that have a high likelihood under the theory. Godfrey-Smith also talks about the relation between beliefs and actions. The discussion is very straightforward and highlights how one can rigorously choose one's actions under uncertainty and maximise expected value using a Bayesian framework. Finally, the section ends by pondering how realistic and useful the Bayesian model is. Indeed, Godfrey-Smith believes it is unreasonable to expect people to hold "exact numbers for their degrees of belief, and ... keep track of the likelihood of all possible bits of evidence, conditional on all the different hypotheses that might be true" (p. 283). However, as intractable as it may sometimes be, we may regard the "Bayesian model ... as a normative model, a model of how you should reason". In conclusion, even if it "does not apply exactly to human minds" (p. 284), Godfrey-Smith considers Subjectivist Bayesianism as a solid normative, if not descriptive, way to model "degrees of belief, the updating of degrees of belief using evidence, and the relation between belief and action" (p. 283).

  • 12.6 Procedures and Experiments

    • Godfrey-Smith looks at eliminative inference, the method of confirming a hypothesis by ruling out all of its seeming alternatives. In finite cases where we can have certainty (about ruling alternatives out), this type of inference can be deductive. However, in scenarios where we only have "less decisive ruling out of alternatives", the Bayesian framework can still be very helpful. Indeed, note that Bayesianism already "handles support in a comparative manner. For one hypothesis to gain some credibility, at least one other hypothesis has to lose some" (p. 285). Godfrey-Smith then shows how to do Bayesian updates when there are more than two hypotheses, but then asks the question of how, in practice, we can "know that we have considered all the relevant alternatives (p. 285)? Consider that in many past debates, scientists did not conceive of our contemporary theories as possibilities. Often a proposed solution is to add "a 'catch-all' or 'cover-all' hypothesis that just says: some thing other than" our known set of hypotheses holds. But then, the question is whether for any given piece of evidence $e$, you can "give a number to '$P(e | \text{something else I haven't thought of})$'? If you can't, you can't use Bayes's theorem to update your beliefs".

    Next, Godfrey-Smith looks at "another general idea about what makes an observation into evidence", namely being embedded within an appropriate procedure. Note, the "ideas that arise here can in many cases be combined with Bayesianism, though they might also provide parts of an alternative picture" (p. 286). What makes a procedure appropriate or good is that it "has the capacity to reliably answer the questions we put to it." For example, a generally good procedure is "using random samples to make inferences about the characteristics of a larger population". Indeed, taking a procedure-based point of view can help with some philosophical problems we have looked at such as the Raven's problem or Goodman's "grue" problem. Godfrey-Smith notes that in most discussions of Raven's problem, "little attention is paid to how the observations of ravens (and shoes) are being collected". However, Godfrey-Smith argues that the underlying procedure can make or break the value of any given observation. For example, if we come across a black raven during a procedure of looking through a collection of black items, we do not get any closer to answering the question of whether all (or most) ravens are black. However, the same black raven, if sampled as a part of a procedure of checking ravens, will have epistemic value for the question. The answer to whether a white shoe is relevant also depends on the underlying sampling procedure (if we are sampling non-raven items, it has no value, but if we are sampling non-black items, it can serve as evidence). It seems that the underlying procedure does not matter only in the case of deductive refutations, e.g. observing a single non-black raven, independently of how or why it was sampled, would refute that all ravens are black. Godfrey-Smith also suggests that thinking about our observational procedure can help somewhat with the "grue" problem. Consider (p. 288):

    inferences made from samples using statistical methods. These methods can be powerful, but they can only be used when some assumptions hold ... these methods cannot be used ... when the act of observing or collecting the data changes the particular objects being observed, in a way that is relevant to the question being asked.

    Indeed, note that "grueness" is an observation-dependent quality. Suppose all of the emeralds we have seen so far have been grue. Importantly, all the observed emeralds have been "affected, with respect to their grueness, by the fact that they had been observed" (p. 289). The same cannot be said for any unobserved emeralds. In turn:

    we cannot extrapolate grueness from the sampled emeralds to the unsampled ones. We cannot do the extrapolation because the observation process has interfered, in an unusual way, with the characteristics of the objects in the sample. This problem does not appear if we want to extrapolate greenness from a sample of emeralds; it only appears if we want to extrapolate grueness ... The grue problem ... is akin to what would be called a confounding variable problem. In a way, Goodman's term 'grue' turns observation (or sampling) itself into a confounding variable. This shows, again, the importance of procedures when thinking about evidence.

    Godfrey-Smith finishes the section with a few remarks. Firstly, he stresses that sampling is not the only way "to investigate generalizations" (p. 290). Indeed many collections "can't be randomly sampled. [Instead, one] can often learn about unobserved cases by making inferences from the internal structure or causal properties of the observed ones. Then randomness plays no role, and more weight falls on the nature of the collection or kind we are studying".
    Next, Godfrey-Smith enquires about what ought to be called an experiment as opposed to being regarded as just an observation. Consider, is "looking up at the sky every night at midnight an experiment, or do you have to manipulate the system you are studying?" In support of non-manipulative observations, Godfrey-Smith notes that "the very idea of a procedure that is followed in collecting data takes us some way from a purely passive or happenstance collection of observations" (p. 290). Also, the discussion on the raven's problem highlights that our underlying procedure can make a big difference as certain hypotheses that can be evidenced only by following the right procedure. Godfrey-Smith deems that the purposefulness, active character and salience of procedural observations warrant regarding them as experiments, albeit very minimal ones. However, as noted in the discussion on interventionism, interventions / manipulations "enables us to control for a lot of potential 'confounding' factors" (p. 291) and to expose or elicit phenomena or dependencies that might otherwise be unidentifiable. "Here we see a role for experiment that requires more than just having a procedure; we have to do things to the system being studied" (p. 291). Godfrey-Smith's conclusion is "that there are some advantages to doing experiments that merely or mostly involve the existence of a procedure, and other advantages that involve more".

  • My comments

    • Throughout the book, Godfrey-Smith highlighted many challenges faced by confirmation, from the holism of testing to the theory-laddenness of observation. The need to interpret and qualify evidence seems to preclude any possibility of observation serving as a neutral and definitive source of truth. Nonetheless, science is meant to try to get nature to bear on theory. Bayesianism promises to be a framework to formalise or even assist in that process. Frankly, though I haven't tried very hard, I can't even conceive of a theory for belief that would be fundamentally different from the Bayesian approach. That is, it seems to me that any account of coherent graded beliefs should also be roughly representable in Bayesian terms.
    However, I think that its generality is really a reflection of how rudimentary the basic Bayesian framework is. Priors, likelihoods, and Bayes' rule for deriving posteriors may (it seems) accommodate any belief framework because they hardly entail any constraints by themselves. As Godfrey-Smith notes, priors and likelihoods can be made to encode so much, from what kinds of theories or explanations we prefer (e.g. internally consistent or simple ones) to how any piece of evidence is to be evaluated with respect to any theory.
    Of course, in practice, there will be the difficult task of compressing and translating all these details into numbers between 0 and 1. There seems to be no obvious general rigorous procedure for this, and so this task presents a considerable challenge in itself.
    I have said that the framework is very general, but perhaps it's better to say that it is flexible enough to deal with many limitations. For example, Godfrey-Smith referenced the problem of dealing with old evidence. Another way of framing this is in terms of how to deal with new hypotheses as they emerge. Here, the standard Bayesian framework would need some additional machinery to deal with the issue, but I think that can be readily crafted. Roughly, we can take our extant and new theories and "reset" their probabilities (perhaps according to some procedure that carefully evaluates a given theory "internally" and / or according to some background assumptions and then adjusts the theory's prior accordingly) and then run them through all of the evidence we have, and obtain a new set of posteriors that now includes our new theories as well. Clearly, this whole process can be repeated over and over. This example is meant to highlight that the Bayesian framework can, in theory, be extended and adapted.
    Though I am not sure how closely we adhere to Bayesian reasoning and rationality in practice, I agree with Godfrey-Smith in that it provides us with good basic conceptual elements and a normative theory for how we should go about adjusting our degrees of belief in light of evidence (and how we should act given our beliefs).
