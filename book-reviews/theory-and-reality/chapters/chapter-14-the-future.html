  • 14.1 Empiricism, Naturalism, and Scientific Realism

    • Godfrey-Smith reviews three -isms he has endorsed in this book. After a brief recap of each of them, he also reviews the role and importance of the social structure of science. On empiricism, he critiques its fixation on induction and generalisation, which historically "took over the discussion of evidence" (p. 323). On a related note, he also criticises empiricism's "attempt to restrict what we can know about and investigate to patterns in experience", deeming that this sort of phenomenalism has been, in part, responsible for the fixation on induction (he does not explain in detail, it stands to reason that phenomenalism discourages taking theoretical objects too seriously, and instead prioritises empirical adequacy i.e. predictive accuracy, hence the focus on induction and generalisation). Godfrey-Smith also warns that this limiting view keeps reappearing, often via frameworks that partition reality into two domains: the accessible "domain of experiences" (p. 324) and the "inaccessible, mysterious" world that lies beyond the mind. Furthermore, most empiricists have in the past neglected the roles of procedures and interventions, and the social side of science, perhaps as empiricism's restrictive set of concepts cannot easily capture such facets of the scientific enterprise. However, Godfrey-Smith does not wish to relegate empiricism to history, rather he interprets it prescriptively as a mindset that "features an ideal of open-mindedness and intellectual flexibility, a distrust of dogmatism, and a grounded, rather than other-worldly, orientation" (p. 325) as opposed to it being a descriptive "general philosophical theory, an account of knowledge in general".

    Godfrey-Smith briefly reiterates his endorsement of scientific realism and naturalism; i.e. the ideas that we are all "parts of the same complex, structured world, learning about that world when things go well" in science (p. 326), and that we should be "willing to draw on our scientific picture when working out how science and other kinds of knowledge work". However, Godfrey-Smith steps away from naturalism when it "falls into a constraining mode" on how philosophy may be done. Godfrey-Smith combines these -isms to produce the following sketch (p. 326-8):

    Humans are biological organisms embedded in a physical world that we evolved to deal with. All our lives - including the most elaborate outgrowths of our social and intellectual lives - involve constant causal traffic and interaction with a larger world in which we are embedded ... From the inside, we can never establish with complete certainty what lies behind a particular sensory input. But looking at ourselves from 'side ways on,' from the point of view taken by biology and psychology, we can ... work out how our perceptual machinery helps us navigate the world ... this point is enough to help us avoid some philosophical problems about our 'access' to the world. We should not think in terms of two domains in reality, one accessible and one mysterious. We are biological systems embedded in a world containing objects ... Our mechanisms of perception and action give us a variety of different kinds of contact with these objects ... This access expands as our technology improves. Parts of the world that must, at one time, be the subject of indirect and speculative inferences can later be much more directly observed, scanned, or assayed ...
    One important point of disagreement is in how people handle the assessment of big ideas - big theories and explanatory hypotheses about the world - as opposed to how they handle everyday life. Maybe our shared biology is enough to make us all fairly empirical when we are trying to get food to eat and work out how to get home. But this does not apply to attempts to develop and justify theories about our overall place in the universe. The scientific strategy is to construct ideas ... in such a way that exposure to experience is sought even in the case of the most general and ambitious hypotheses about the world ... one looks to experience as a constraint on hypotheses. In science, an acceptance of the provisional status of big ideas is at least part of the ideology - part of the official mindset that one is supposed to have ... In other forms of intellectual culture, especially where tradition has a stronger role, this is not so. We can distinguish this general scientific strategy from a particular way of organizing how the strategy is carried out. In a broad sense, science is the attempt to assess large-scale ideas by exposing them to experience. The Scientific Revolution and the work that followed it developed a particular, socially organized way of carrying out the strategy. The term 'science' can also be used, more narrowly, to refer to that social organization.

    The section ends with a review of the social organisation of science. Though Godfrey-Smith contends that, hypothetically, scientific research could be done by a single individual, in practice, via "the development of institutional structures, reward systems, and informal norms, a particular way of socially organizing empirical investigation has developed" (p. 328). Furthermore, Godfrey-Smith distinguishes between "two dimensions of science's social organization - the organization of work at a time and the organization of work across times" (p. 329). Looking into the former reveals the present "reward structure, the mix of competition and cooperation, norms of criticism, and the like" (p. 328), while looking into the latter reveals "ways of transmitting ideas across time and (again) a reward system that makes it worthwhile to carry on where earlier workers left off" as "[scientific] work is cumulative". However, Godfrey-Smith notes that this cumulative structure was not always a given, and that historically across many fields of enquiry, there were periods of sustained work, stasis and even regression, until, "field by field, this haphazard pattern was replaced by more cumulative work" (p. 329). Regarding "the organization of scientific work at a time, the middle of the seventeenth century may be crucially important ... this period saw work directed at setting up a culture of controlled criticism and a new kind of network of trust. This side of science's social organization was deliberately shaped, rather than arising by happenstance". In this context, Godfrey-Smith briefly talks about alchemy, and how Boyle himself contrasted the "open, cooperative new scientific culture" he helped foster with the secrecy and mysticism of alchemy.
    Now, while such social accounts of science elucidate how science works across many areas where empiricism falls short, Godfrey-Smith still holds that we need to retain "some basic empiricist ideas about the constraint of hypotheses by observation" (p. 330). There still needs to be a "willingness to be guided by data, rather than merely using data as an argumentative resource". Finally, Godfrey-Smith also stresses that "scientists are people, and people are often full of biases", yet science doesn't depend on all scientists being fully fair-minded; rather, the "more important thing is the extent to which the norms and habits of scientific behavior give the process a self correcting character. If bias induces error, there will usually be something to gain from showing it; there is a process by which correction will come."

  • 14.2 Another Kind of Scientific Change

    • This last section looks into some of the ways science has been changing in recent years (at level 2), largely as a result of its interaction with broader society (level 3). These changes apply not only to "the theories people believe and the technologies they use, but in its organization and patterns of work" (p. 331). Kuhn, in his era in the 20th century conceived of science as being a "rather insulated and self-governing activity". But this assessment was made during the cold war, when there was anxiety "about falling behind the Soviets. The result was a high level of support and a practice of not worrying too much about how creative people would use that support. Self-propelled normal science was a natural result". In contrast (p. 331-2):

    Contemporary science has more to-and-fro with business and the rest of culture, and more oversight and guidance from governments, funding bodies, and administrators of many kinds. The links between science and business, especially in molecular biology and now in nanotechnology, lead to a lot more money going into the pockets of scientists. Many of them start companies to commercialize their work. In maintaining research, writing grant applications is now continual and onerous; it cannot be assumed that support will continue if a lab does good work. There was always oversight, but a light touch has been replaced with a heavier one.

    Some think that these changes have led to more democratisation in science: due to "competitive and ubiquitous grant-writing processes, funding has less inertia and more mobility" (p. 332), and this has weakened "old-boy" networks. However, on the flip side, scientists can spend up to 20%, if not 50% of their time and energy on grant applications. Another theme Godfrey-Smith deems concerning is the weakening of the "tradition of rather unconstrained inquiry, of scientists 'following their noses' without worrying too much about practical applications for their work or externally set priorities" (p. 332). The alleged practical side of this tradition is "that if you let people follow their noses, they will find out things that will eventually be very useful, though we can't predict where they will be found" (p. 333). Godfrey-Smith then gives the electric motor, CRISPR and number theory as examples to support this claim, and believes that free inquiry will suffer as "limited oversight is trusted less than it was, and universities and government bodies engage in closer scrutiny. There is more demand for commercial connections and for work to align with declared priorities" (p. 334). That said, Godfrey-Smith also opines that some constraints on science could be good, and gives the example of animal testing, which he thinks is being excessively used. Finally, Godfrey-Smith discusses "the possibility that there is more misconduct, fraud, and careless work than there used to be". It is claimed that "the kind of competition that scientists are now encouraged or required to engage in is one where sheer speed, and also a kind of showiness, count for a lot, and these incentives" allegedly drive the malign behaviour. However, Godfrey-Smith thinks that the data on this is somewhat mixed, and that the apparent trends may have to do with increased oversight, and that such worries, e.g. about an increase in fraud, have been around for many decades. Nonetheless, Godfrey-Smith does think that there seems to be, to some degree, an ongoing reproducibility crisis "not due primarily to misconduct, but to carelessness, cutting corners, and, perhaps, overly relaxed statistical standards ... Here the sheer pace of contemporary science, and the role of high-impact publications in generating prestige and support, may play an important role. Heesen (2018), using a mathematical model ... argues that ... the benefit to an individual from speedy publication is a key factor" (p. 335). Godfrey-Smith thinks that this will be a "difficult problem to fix". Still, he concludes that it:

    would be fruitless to try to turn the clock back, even if it were generally desirable. What we need to do is think as intelligently as we can about how science arose, how it gained its strengths and weaknesses, and how we might shape it, as well as utilize it, in a positive way. The philosophy of science is part of this.

  • My comments

    • I find myself broadly agreeing with Godfrey-Smith on his positions regarding the three -isms. Perhaps this is because I have not looked enough into the opposing literature (though I doubt that is a significant reason). It may also be because Godfrey-Smith's views are simply the most common ones, that is, the positions people "naturally" tend towards given our current milieu. In any case, it seems too far-fetched to posit that our best scientific theories tell us nothing true about reality, or that we may reasonably ignore scientific findings when philosophising, or that in seeking truth, we need not constrain our big theories about the world by what observation tells us. To be clear, I like adventurous ideas and challenging the status quo, but I believe that if you do so, you have to intensively engage with science and its findings, and to "succeed", your story needs to be more convincing than what the current "sciences" (and the philosophical views that are sympathetic to them) say.

    For example, Latour's (and other sociologists') claim that "evidence" is merely a rhetorical resource in arguments, as opposed to an objective constraint on theory, seems like an extraordinary posit that demands but does not receive extraordinary support. Its logical conclusion seems to be that pretty much any theory or belief can be as true and probable as any other, given all our data and observations. However, I have never come across any convincing arguments for this position; the talking points I keep hearing about point to how our current theories suffer from anomalies, and to how our older theories ended up being "wrong", and so we should expect that our current theories will be invalidated someday too (hence, we can already pre-emptively reject them). However, both of these points also seem very much compatible with notions like "partial" truth, a convergence to truth, and the idea that some theories can be more or less be accurate/true than others.

    As another example: I somewhat doubt common-sense realism, that is, the idea that there really is a common physical world that we all inhabit. My rough and vague intuition is that it's surely unlikely that we, as limited creatures, have detailed access to the "fundamental" structure of reality. But it also seems to me that our modern theories provide the best approximations we have regarding what that structure is like. In any case, any future theory that reveals a different structure will also have to explain why common-sense realism works so well as an approximation, one can't just ignore and not account for this appearance. I suppose this is an empiricist position, i.e. insofar as it is a stipulation for empirical adequacy. However, unlike some empiricists, such as von Fraassen (and in agreement with Godfrey-Smith), I don't see any convincing arguments as to why the theoretical objects of our best scientific theories can't be real (to some extent). I do acknowledge that truth is very difficult to make sense of, and this notion of "partial" truth I keep appealing to is perhaps even more vague. I gather that there is no fully convincing or practical philosophical account of how exactly one should think of these concepts, but I would insist that just because there isn't a definitive account, it doesn't follow that the concepts are invalid. The idea of truth, in the sense of how things really are, has a deep intuitive appeal and cannot, I think, be easily dismissed. There is something right about the sentences "there is a table in my living room" and "there is no helicopter in my living room", something that isn't shared with the sentence "there are fifty goats roaming around in my living room"; and why shouldn't this idea of right and wrong extend to bigger ideas about what the world is like in terms of content and structure?

    Finally, on a different note, it does seem that the pressure to continually publish and to gain credit can come at the expense of good and diligent scientific ideas and work, but I get an impression that this worry can sometimes get overblown (and is field dependent). Also, with the rise of AI and the increasing pace of technological advancement, it would seem that the rate of scientific output may increase far more still. However, I think it does not follow that the quality of scientific work must suffer; indeed, if these tools empower cognition and data analysis, greater scientific achievements may come within our reach. I also optimistically think that automation and the excesses of intrusive technology may afford and encourage a conscious turn among some people and researchers towards more deliberate, slow, deep "offline" thinking practice, and that this somewhat reactionary turn may function as a complementary natural counterweight to the fast-paced technological framework that is emerging. This deliberate mode of thinking may then serve as an incubator for future creative and deep ideas about the world, the sort of ideas which cannot be derived via a few AI prompts.
