  • 11.1 Knowing Why

    • We often appeal to science for explanations of why things happen. Yet, some philosophers (especially empiricists), and even some scientists, are wary of this, for perhaps all we can ever get from science are predictions (though, perhaps an explanation is just that: a good prediction). We may also ponder how explanation and evidence relate. Can they be approached separately, e.g. can we first, on the basis of evidence, select a theory, and then use the theory to produce explanations? Maybe not, for maybe the two notions are too interdependent, as theories "are often preferred by scientists because they seem to yield good explanations of puzzling phenomena" (p. 247), which "suggests that there is a close relation between the problem of analyzing explanation and the problem of analyzing evidence" (p. 248). In any case, chapter 11 focuses on explanations, and chapter 12 returns to the topic of evidence. Furthermore:

    Two other topics that are closely linked to explanation are causation and laws of nature. Perhaps to explain something is to describe its causes, or the laws behind it. But what are causes and laws?

  • 11.2 The Rise and Fall of the Covering Law Theory of Explanation

    • The Logical Positivists rejected metaphysical takes on scientific explanation. By the time they moderated and became the Logical Empiricists, Hempel and Oppenheim tried to promote an empiricism-friendly take on explanation called "the covering law theory of explanation" (p. 249):

    The basic ideas of the covering law theory are simple. First, to explain something is to show how to derive it in a logical argument. The explanandum will be the conclusion of the argument, and the premises are the explanans. A good explanation must first of all be a good logical argument, but in addition, the premises must contain at least one statement of a law of nature. The law must make a real contribution to the argument; it cannot be something merely tacked on ... Some of the arguments that express explanations will be deductively valid, but this is not required in all cases. The covering law theory was intended to allow that some good explanations could be expressed as nondeductive arguments ("inductive" arguments, in the logical empiricists' broad sense of the term). If we can take a particular phenomenon and embed it within an argument in which the premises include a law and bestow high probability on the conclusion, this yields a good explanation of the phenomenon.

    Importantly, note that "a law of nature, for a logical empiricist, was not supposed to be something very grandiose. It was supposed to be a kind of basic regularity, a pattern, in the flow of events" (p. 250). Also, "for the covering law theory, there is not much difference between explanation and prediction. To predict something, we use an argument to try to show that it is to be expected, though we don't know for sure yet whether it is going to happen. When we explain something, we know that it has happened, and we show that it could have been predicted using an argument containing a law" (p. 250). However, by rendering explanation and prediction as almost equivalent, the covering law theory exposes itself to a powerful argument called the asymmetry problem, well illustrated via "the case of the flagpole and the shadow" (p. 250-1):

    Suppose we have a flagpole casting a shadow on a sunny day. Someone asks: why is the shadow $X$ meters long? According to the covering law theory, we can give a good explanation of the shadow by deducing the length of the shadow from the height of the flagpole, the position of the sun, the laws of optics, and some trigonometry. We can show why that length of shadow was to be expected, given the laws and the circumstances. The argument can even be made deductively valid. So far, so good. The problem is that we can run just as good an argument in another direction. Just as we can deduce the length of the shadow from the height of the pole (plus optics and trigonometry), we can deduce the height of the pole from the length of the shadow (and the same laws). An equally good argument, logically speaking, can be run in both directions; either can give information about the other. But it seems that we cannot run an equally good explanation in both directions, though the covering law theory says we can ... What we find here is that explanations have a kind of directionality ... Once this point is seen, it becomes obvious and devastating.

    The asymmetry problem can also be well illustrated via the example of some disease $D$ and a given symptom $S$. The fact that "it seems that good explanations of $S$ can be given in terms of $D$ even if $S$ is not a very reliable symptom of $D$" (p. 251) further highlights the gap that there seems to be between explanation and prediction, and so further exposes the shortcomings of the covering law theory. Still, the most devastating blow seems to come from the asymmetry problem, as the problem of unreliable symptoms can be somewhat dealt with by appealing to the idea that covering law style explanations can be non-deductive, i.e. probabilistic/predictive. In any case, it's worthwhile to note that the asymmetry problem was anticipated yet dismissed by Hempel himself. His view "was to accept that if his theory allowed explanations to run in two directions in cases where it seems that explanation only runs in one direction, then both directions must be OK" (p. 252), but the seeming absurdity of this position (e.g. of explaining a viral infection as being the result of a characteristic symptom) has led to it, and to covering law theory, being generally rejected.

  • 11.3 Causation and Unification

    • This section is well summarised by its final paragraph (p. 258):

    the covering law theory is dead, and its death was informative. In its wake, we need not look for a new theory of some single relation or pattern that is involved in all scientific explanation. Very often, causation is involved. The same goes for unification and for deriving phenomena from laws. But different fields have different concepts and standards of explanation.

    In more detail, "as people abandoned the covering law theory [due to deep issues such as the asymmetry problem], the main proposal about explanation discussed, in different ways, was the idea that explaining something is giving information about how it was caused" (p. 252). In particular, we will "need to know what kind of information about causes makes for a good explanation". Though we can imagine an "idealized 'complete' explanation that contains everything in the causal history of the event to be explained, specified in total detail", only "some piece or pieces of the complete explanation will be relevant" and a good (causal) explanation then will be a "description of these relevant pieces of the whole." However, not everyone is on-board with this approach, as "for many philosophers, [especially empiricists,] causation is a suspicious metaphysical concept that we do best to avoid when trying to understand science" (p. 251), for it can seem like a "hidden connection between things, unobservable but essential to the operation of the universe" (p. 251-2).

    In large part due to wariness about causation, a notable alternative to the causal approach has been developed, with its central idea being "that explanation should be analyzed in terms of unification". Friedman (1974) and Kitcher (1981 and 1989) have been key figures, but the idea of explanation as unification already "was a sort of 'unofficial' theory of explanation within ... logical empiricism" (p. 254) and presents a much better approach, Godfrey-Smith reckons, than their "official" covering law theory:

    The unificationist theory holds that explanation in science is a matter of connecting a diverse set of facts by subsuming them under a set of basic patterns and principles. Science constantly strives to reduce the number of things that we must accept as fundamental. We try to develop general explanatory schemes that can be applied as widely as possible ... some very famous theories - Darwin's theory of evolution and Newton's later work on the nature of matter - were compelling to scientists in their early stages despite not making many new predictions, because they promised to explain so much. And this 'explanatory promise' seems to have been the ability of those theories to unify a great range of phenomena with a few general principles.

    An important question for those, such as Kitcher, who have tried to give an account of explanation purely in terms of unification without resorting to causation, is whether their approach can handle the asymmetry problem. Kitcher has argued that it is feasible and that any "causal talk is really a loose summary of more basic asymmetries that involve unification" (p. 254). Godfrey-Smith does not elaborate on this interesting point, but I will in my comments.

    What Godfrey-Smith does go on to discuss however, are his pluralist views, arguing that "we do not have to choose" and that "[nothing] stops us from holding that a variety of different relations can be explanatory" (p. 255). Note, Godfrey-Smith does not advocate for a form of pluralism where "a good explanation is something that satisfies either the causal test or the unification test", as such an approach may overlook the fact that "different scientific fields will establish their own criteria for what will pass as a good explanation". Instead, in line with his naturalism, he advocates for "a kind of contextualism - a view that treats the standards for good explanation as partially dependent on the scientific context". This position seems close to Kuhn's view that different paradigms bring with them their own standards, and that, in particular, "standards about whether a relation counts as 'causal' also depend on paradigms". Godfrey-Smith notes that (p. 256):

    [to] support his claims, Kuhn focused on Newton's theory of gravity. Does Newton's theory explain the falling of objects, given that Newton's treatment of gravity gave no intuitive mechanism but only a mathematical relationship? Some people answered no, but over time it became part of Newtonianism that the right kind of mathematical law does count as an explanation.

    Building on Kuhn's point, Godfrey-Smith notes that it therefore "would be a mistake to conclude that no explanations have the form described by the covering law theory". Ultimately, "views about explanation will depend on views about what the world contains", and this meshes well with the idea that scientific traditions can determine their own standards for what counts as good explanation. Also, although some standards for explanation will be rigorous and technical, and may be defined in terms of causation or unification, in other instances, they may be more informal. Often, "descriptions of phenomena", which I interpret as descriptions of the entities involved in processes or phenomena (Godfrey-Smith does not clarify) count as explanations. I will come back to this point that explanations can be descriptive in my comments.

    To conclude the section, Godfrey-Smith elaborates in two ways about his views. First, he contrasts his rejection of explanation as "some single, special relation common to all of science" with how Van Fraassen rejects the same thesis. Van Fraassen deems explanation to be something wholly external to science, which aims to achieve empirical adequacy, and not to explain why things happen. This contrasts with Godfrey-Smith position, which holds that "explanation is thoroughly internal to science, but variously so. Assessments of what explains what are an important part of scientific reasoning, but different fields may use somewhat different concepts and standards of explanation" (p. 257).
    Secondly, Godfrey-Smith revisits abduction, which is also known as inference to the best explanation. The way Godfrey-Smith sees it, abduction "is a matter of devising and comparing hypotheses about hidden structures that might be responsible for data" (p. 258), yet since Godfrey-Smith holds that the standards of explanation can vary across scientific fields and periods, he rejects the usage of "inference to the best explanation" as a term for it, as what is considered best may vary. Instead, he advocates for the term "explanatory inference", which does not suggest that there could be "a single measure of explanatory goodness, which applies to all of science".

  • 11.4 Laws, Causes, and Interventions

    • This section compares and contrasts the notions of laws and causation. Thereafter, it also takes a good look at the interventionist approach to causation. Both laws of nature and causation "seem aimed at picking out a special kind of connection between things in the world. Causation is sometimes called, half jokingly, 'the cement of the universe'" (p. 258). In turn, some sceptical philosophers have tried to work without these concepts, while others have (p. 258-9):

    tried to reconstrue them in a very low-key way ('Yes, there is causation, but it is no more than this ...'). In particular, philosophers have tried to analyze both laws and causation in terms of patterns in the arrangement of things, rather than some extra connection between things. Sometimes this project is referred to as 'Humeanism' ... Present-day Humeans ... want to avoid believing in any sort of unobservable cement connecting the universe together ... A philosopher following this approach will try to construe laws of nature as no more than regularities, or basic patterns, in the arrangement of events. To treat laws of nature in this way is to leave behind one of the familiar connotations of the term 'law.' Usually, we see laws as directing, guiding, or governing in some way. In this spirit, laws of nature can be seen as governing the flow of events and hence responsible for the regular patterns that we see, rather than being identical with those patterns ... Those following the Humean approach see a 'governing' conception of laws as a seduction that must be avoided.

    Indeed, there has been a growing recognition, or at least a belief, that the "'laws of physics' do not really describe the behavior of real systems at all, but only the behavior of highly idealized or fictional systems" (p. 259). Furthermore, "another important change that resulted from a closer look at actual science is that philosophers are no longer obsessed with natural laws as the goal of scientific theorizing". This is largely due to the science of biology, which has defied an outdated view wherein a scientific field is oriented around hypothesising on and organising its ideas in terms of a set of natural laws. Now:

    the topic of causation has generated debates that are sometimes similar to debates about laws. On one side, we have those who see causation as no more than a kind of regular pattern in the flow of events. On the other side are those who see causation as a connection between events - between the ball hitting the window and the glass breaking - that is responsible for the patterns.

    However, Godfrey-Smith alleges that there is a difference here between the case of causation and the case of laws, in that for "causal relations, it's possible to argue that there are indeed hidden connections between things, but not so hidden that they can't be studied directly by physics" (p. 260). Godfrey-Smith does not elaborate here, but he is likely referring to attempts to define causation in terms of the transfer of "marks", momentum, energy, force or other continuous physical quantities.

    In any case, the rest of the section looks into an alternative way of formalising causation, namely in terms of interventions. In the so-called interventionist framework, we typically specify a system via a set of variables that correspond to factors that describe the system and that can vary. Godfrey-Smith does not point this out, but it's important for the variables to be meaningfully/semantically distinct. Now, "imagine being able to reach in and change the state of just one variable or one 'node' in the network. When you reach in and change the state of [e.g.] $A$, if you find that [say] $B$ changes (or becomes more likely), you have found that $A$ is a cause of $B$. Causal analysis, on this view, is analysis of what follows from interventions, whether those interventions are real or imagined" (p. 260-1). Note that the "intervention is supposed to be a kind of 'surgical' change - we change $A$ alone and see what ensues" (p. 261). This ensures that whatever changes we see in $B$ are due to the change made to the factor that $A$ tracks. Importantly, we do not infer causation from looking at $A$ passively and seeing whether $B$ covaries, for there can be a confounding factor, measurable by, say, a variable $C$, that influences both $A$ and $B$, and the resultant coordination could then be mistaken as a sign of $A$ influencing $B$ (or vice versa). However, the external intervention on $A$ is precisely what cuts off any such confounding causal influence and can let us see if controlled variation in $A$ corresponds with any changes in $B$. Indeed, it should not be too surprising that "interventions can unearth causal relationships in a way that non-experimental data often cannot" (p. 261). Nevertheless, sometimes when we have enough background knowledge (e.g. suppose we know that there can be no confounder $C$ and that $A$ temporally precedes $B$), we can make inferences about causation just from passively observed conditional probabilities. The rest of the section looks at some examples of causal inference and ends with a few remarks about interventionism (p. 263):

    Interventionism is philosophically unusual in several ways ... The idea of an imagined intervention was used above. Do we need to purge the theory of any references to the imaginary or merely possible? In addition, interventionism ... includes a kind of circularity. When we try to say what a causal relation is within this framework, we can't get away from using language that is causal itself. An intervention is itself a special kind of causal process, not merely an arrangement of events.

    Yet, its proponents do not seem too worried; they believe they are making progress and need not solve every philosophical issue at hand. Their framework also suggests that maybe "the whole idea of causation is dependent on a certain point of view, the point of view of an agent who is reaching into a system (or imagining doing so) from the outside" (p. 264). That is, for the agent to be independent, they must in some sense be outside of the reach of forces operating in the world, but curiously they must also be in close contact with the world if they are to intervene in it. So "perhaps there is a kind of mental 'stepping outside,' an unusual way of conceiving of things, that is part of causal thinking" (p. 264).

  • My comments

    • Being rather immersed in the causal picture makes the unificationist account intriguing to me, as it seems to involve some fundamental ideas that are not reducible to standard causal talk. The idea that an explanation is attained when a particular can be identified as an instance of some general pattern/scheme out of a set of carefully chosen patterns/schemes that has been made as small as possible seems deep and not inherently causal. Indeed, it seems to present a theoretically independent way of making sense of explanation via general schemes that can side-step dealing with anything in explicitly causal terms. What's more, one can imagine that there could be schemes that are non-causal (or perhaps evne anti-causal), e.g. relating to the structural or combinatorial properties of systems, or to principles (e.g. conservation laws) that are not formulated in causal terms. Likewise, one can, in principle, imagine an instance of some system that follows a very elaborate causal structure, such that it does not correspond to any common scheme/pattern that would be in a given explanatory set. However, basic and modular causal structures or mechanisms, that let us handle many diverse what-if scenarios, appear to be very good candidates for both unificationist approaches and causal approaches to explanation. Yet, while there may be much overlap between the approaches (as some of the best candidate schemes/patterns for unification probably are common modular basic causal structures), it stands to reason that the two approaches do not have to yield the same "explanatory set".

    Now, a question that Godfrey-Smith did not explicitly address is how come the flagpole example (and the asymmetry problem in general) is something that unificationist theory can handle. Perhaps it comes down to the fact that, outside of idealised 2D instances of the problem, the backwards / inverse problem is very under-constrained / underdetermined. In turn, the same set of small assumptions and rules that let us derive the forward case will not allow us to solve the inverse problem, unless we add additional non-general assumptions / constraints to the "reverse scheme" (e.g. additional constraints about the geometry of the material casting the shadow), hence the backwards scheme, by failing to be as simple and general, will not be an element of a unificationist explanatory set, and so there will be no analogous "backwards explanation". Furthermore, I think that the argumentation here can be made entirely without any recourse to any notion of causation, which I find fascinating, even though the problem does present a scenario where the operating causal mechanisms (relating to optics and the interaction of light and materials) present generalisable predictive patterns that are effective as explanations in the forward direction but not in the reverse anti-causal direction.

    On a different note, I wish to make a point about Godfrey-Smith's comment on how many "low-key" explanations in science simply amount to "descriptions of phenomena" (p. 257). I think Godfrey-Smith's phrasing understates the importance of such forms of constitutive or "descriptive explanations". Perhaps descriptive explanations given in terms of the theoretical entities of scientific theories are more prone to change than causal explanations or explanations in terms of unifying schemes (as scientific theories are revised) but they seem no less important. In fact, almost every typical scientific explanation I can think of involves some appeal to the theoretical entities of a relevant theory. For example, any explanation of how the sun produces light and heat, whether it appeals to "unifying" principles about nuclear fusion, or whether it presents a causal account of the steps involved in propagating the core's energy to the surface where it can radiate out, will likely appeal to entities such as hydrogen, helium, photons and processes such as random walks and convection. So really, "description" is a part of any explanation, and in many cases takes centre stage over and above talk about causation or unifying. schemes. Indeed, explanation often means taking a process or object of interest, and casting it in terms of the mechanisms and entities of some scientific theory.
