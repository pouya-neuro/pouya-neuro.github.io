Chapter 13 — Truth, Simplicity, and Other Problems

Summary goes here.

  • 13.1 The Problem of Truth

    • This chapter grapples with the concept of truth and the related topics of model building, consensus and Occam's razor. The section notes that uncovering truth seems to be the goal of science, but it is hard to make sense of what exactly that means. Indeed, some "inscriptions, diagrams, and computer files have something about them that enables good bridges and effective drugs to be made, while other inscriptions (etc.) lead people astray" (p. 294), yet Godfrey-Smith notes that (p. 295):

    it might be an error to put the power so much in those sentences, equations, and so on themselves. Clearly a great deal depends on the interpretation of those things. If a person is given the sentences alone, without knowing how to interpret them, nothing useful can be done. Technological successes seem to require a pair of things: the right representations and an ability to interpret them.

    Also, one:

    might say the goal of theories is to predict events only, not represent the workings of nature. That does not entirely remove the role of truth. To make a prediction is to say something about what we will see or hear, and to make a good prediction is to say something true about what we will see or hear. The idea of truth has not disappeared yet, though it might eventually.

  • 13.2 Correspondence, Coherence, and Usefulness

    • Godfrey-Smith looks "at some ... [standard] ideas about truth, not ideas about science, but about language in general ... that are ... direct attempts to say what truth is" (p. 296). Specifically, he looks at "correspondence views, coherence views, and pragmatist views":

    correspondence views pursue the idea that truth is a match of some sort between a belief or theory and the way things are. Perhaps truth is a sort of picturing ... Scientific theories might be, in some abstract sense, pictures or maps of the world.

    Godfrey-Smith notes that this view can get tied up with the troubled idea of facts:

    People sometimes talk of a fact is as a piece of the world itself, but sometimes a fact is seen as something like a true sentence. The word unhelpfully occupies both sides of the relationship we want to understand - the relationship between representations and the world they are aimed at.

    Godfrey-Smith then mentions a common criticism (of the correspondence view), namely that "we can never hold up our beliefs and the world itself, as separate things, and compare them to see whether there is a correspondence between them". However, Godfrey-Smith is not impressed by this criticism, for the claim that you can't verify some correspondence doesn't entail that it doesn't exist. Also, a belief's usefulness for prediction and actions could potentially be taken as evidence that it corresponds to its target. Indeed, Godfrey-Smith believes that a more serious problem is that it is "hard to see what this correspondence relation might actually be. 'Picturing' is a mere metaphor, one applicable in simple cases but not helping much with most cases, especially in science" (p. 297).

    Moving on, we have views that characterise truth as "a kind of coherence between beliefs themselves":

    this initially seems rather far from truth, because coherence is a relation between one belief and others, not between beliefs and a world they are aimed at. But there are ways to motivate coherence views ... truth is ... something we search for ... How do we do that? Coherence is what we can actually work on in our belief systems, something we can aim for. It is also something whose presence we can discern for ourselves (though not infallibly) ... coherence seems to fit the role of truth ... Isn't observational data something ... a constraint that goes beyond 'coherence'? In reply, it can be argued that an observation plays this role only when it has been described and recorded, and then it is just another part of a web of beliefs that may either be coherent or not.

    I personally find a coherence view, by itself, untenable. Consider that we can conceive of, and refine an ever more internally consistent fantasy world filled with elves and dragons. Would patching our story's plot holes make elves any more real? At best, if we presuppose that reality itself is "coherent", then coherence can serve as a useful heuristic, but we can't really rule out that reality itself may be inconsistent/incoherent. Therefore, I hold that coherence cannot replace correspondence. In any case, before moving on to pragmatist views on truth, Godfrey-Smith notes that coherence can be a matter of degree, which may suggest that truthfulness also comes in degrees (if we relate the two notions).

    One of the two main historical pragmatist views on truth identifies it with "what would be believed at a sort of ideal limit of scientific investigation" (p. 298). Granted a correspondence view, we can come to a similar position (although pragmatism conflates the representation with the represented) if we also admit some notion of progress or convergence in science. The other of the two main historical pragmatist views would be that "true beliefs are ones that tend to lead us to successful actions". Yet, what does this view make of beliefs that are only somewhat or occasionally useful? Pragmatists such as "James accepted this and said that a belief can be a bit true, or true for me but not you, or true in some contexts". Along similar lines, Godfrey-Smith also contends "that a belief could work well for someone despite being false". Though this point, as Godfrey-Smith puts it, seems shaky since a pragmatist would retort that they define truth in terms of use (so we would also need to appeal to a non-pragmatic notion of truth, e.g. pragmatist views may allow for truths that are incoherent, which we might oppose if we appeal to coherence). Indeed, I think that Godfrey-Smith makes the right move by returning to a correspondence-centred view and accounting for a belief's usefulness for "successful actions" as being a product of its truthfulness, as opposed to the pragmatist view that seems to "have things the wrong way round".

  • 13.3 An Indirect Approach, via Ramsey

    • Godfrey-Smith looks at Ramsey's approach to truth in terms of satisfaction conditions, that is, in terms of "a simple relation between what is said and the way things are" (p. 299), for "truth is what you have when the satisfaction conditions of what you say are actually satisfied". This can be interpreted as a correspondence view in a "minimal, low-key sense", though "correspondence here does not involve picturing, or anything like that". The challenge for this view is "explaining what it is to make a definite claim about how things are, what it is to say something that has a satisfaction condition", for truth "is easy once we have made sense of assertion, and that first task is not easy". Godfrey-Smith also mentions "deflationism or minimalism", according to which "there is no philosophical problem of truth at all. To say "X is true" is just to say X. Truth is not a special and hard-to-analyze relation between words and the world". He notes that (p. 300):

    Ramsey's view is sometimes seen as early statement of deflationism. In a way it is, as he does deflate the problem of truth itself. But this is only because he thinks the hard part is explaining what it is to make a claim or assertion.

    The text itself does not clarify, but I think it's safe to say that even though truth claims can be "deflated", ("it is true that it is raining" simply means "it is raining"), it still holds (in a typical deflationist position) that any claim about the world will depend on how the world actually is. We can't "deflate" away the world. Moving on, Godfrey-Smith acknowledges that Ramsey's view has to contend with many of the problems discussed throughout the book (p. 300-1):

    Perhaps it is a mistake about the nature of language to suppose that people say things that have satisfaction conditions tying those claims to definite situations in the world. Perhaps it is only possible to talk about our experiences, or perhaps we can only 'aim' our assertions at vague clumps of the world rather than at photons or cats in particular. What we need next, then, is a theory of whether and how it is possible to make claims about specific aspects of the world ... that covers ... mathematical formulas, diagrams, and so on - I will call all these 'representational devices.' ... There are some detailed philosophical theories ... I don't think any of these theories work very well ... But we do know roughly what is involved. We have, first, a mass of practices of creating representations and using them ... Second, there are causal relations that link these practices and the representations themselves to various other parts of the natural world, sometimes with the aid of elaborate instruments ... And third, there is commentary that people engage in, using one representational device to say something about another ... a good theory must give a role to all of these.

    So even if we adopt Ramsey's approach, there will still be many challenges for any general theory of truth. Godfrey-Smith goes on to discuss further relevant questions (p. 301-2):

    What becomes of the debates about whether truth is relative or absolute ... ? If there is no 'objective' reality - no way the world is, regardless of perspective - then truth also will be relative to perspective. Truth will be correspondence to the perspective-dependent objects ... Another possibility is that the relativity of truth ... might stem from the other side of the relationship, the side of assertions or representations. If it's not possible to say something with definite satisfaction conditions, and 'what is said' is merely a matter of interpretation, then whether what was said is true or false will also be a matter of interpretation.

    The section ends with a brief discussion on "the practical value of truth" (p. 302) in guiding actions, and on how we "develop elaborate ways of expressing scientific claims - ways of categorizing things, ways of organizing ideas ... including mathematical formalisms, schemata for diagrams, and the structures within computer programs" (p. 302-3). Such representational devices may have a "map-like relation to the part of the world [they are] aimed at dealing with" (p. 303). So, it "might be tempting to push this idea further and say that many, or all, special-purpose symbolisms introduced in science are maps, in an abstract sense". However, Godfrey-Smith argues that this would be an overstatement. To support his case, Godfrey-Smith points to an alternative use: "[representational] systems in science often enable the transformation and manipulation of ideas, and that is a power they have that does not require that they 'map' anything. An example is provided by mathematical symbols. These are means for making assertions, but they also facilitate the manipulation of information, by substitutions and transformations".

  • 13.4 Models

    • In many cases of modelling, "what we seem to do is first specify a structure, an arrangement, and see how it will behave. Then we use those results to draw conclusions about the actual world ... [A model] is similar to the real world in various ways, but easier to comprehend. This combination - close to reality but simpler - makes it informative" (p. 304). So, "we describe and explore a model system, and then we compare it to a target system we are trying to understand" (p. 304), and sometimes:

    it seems that we start with the real world and imagine away some complications - this is also called idealization. In other cases, it seems that what we are doing is constructing a fictional or hypothetical scenario from scratch, and comparing it to the real world once we understand it.

    Now, as a point of contrast, Godfrey-Smith suggests that in Darwin's On the Origin of Species, "there is no attempt to detour through a simplified system. Instead he just tries to describe what is going on and gives evidence for his view" (p. 305). This and Mendeleev's periodic table are presented as examples of descriptive works that are not model-like (p. 305). I am not sure I agree with this. Evolution by natural selection posits a general mechanism, and the periodic table posits a structure, and for both, we appeal to theoretical entities (species or atoms). All of this put together seems like modelling to me, though I can also imagine how one could define modelling more narrowly and require more explicit theoretical commitments regarding entities and processes.

    In any case, this "idea of comparing imaginary systems to real ones can be philosophically puzzling" (p. 305). It can help to hone in on the mathematical aspects of models, which "might be thought of as attempts to mirror some of the dependence relationships that exist between the parts of a real system" (p. 306). That is, "a network of dependence relationships can be constructed, and the result can be compared to the dependence relationships in a real-world target". However, Godfrey-Smith stresses that not all models are mathematical, e.g. consider actual physical scale models of larger more complex systems. Godfrey-Smith also notes that "computer models are an interesting case, and in some ways they seem to lie 'between' mathematical or imagined models and physically built ones". Indeed, according to one view of computer modelling, suggested by Weisberg (2013), the aim is "to specify a set of procedural or algorithmic dependencies, and then claim that these are similar to some relationships that hold in the real world". Alternatively, we may simply view computers as "an aid to the scientific imagination. Our ability to specify interesting systems and arrangements outruns our ability to work out what they would do if they were real. The computer, with its precise and regular operation, helps us with that." (p. 307).

    Godfrey-Smith moves on to the topic of truth vis-à-vis models. Godfrey-Smith deems that "[a] model itself is not true or false" (p. 307), yet "when we model something, we also express theoretical hypotheses. These are expressed in language and compare the system to the real world: 'This model resembles an actual physical pendulum in the following ways....' Those claims can be true or false". We may then talk about accuracy, which can come in degrees. However, the discussion will have to be a bit nuanced, for different "people can use the same model while interpreting it differently. One person might use the model as a predictive device, something that gives an output when you plug in specific inputs, without caring how the inner workings of the model relate to those of the target. Another person might treat the same model as a picture of the causal structure inside a system being studied".

    The mention of causal dependencies brings us to the final point of the discussion on how "models give us new conditional statements, statements of the form 'If there is a setup of kind $X$, then it will do $Y$.' Yet, granted that models simplify or modify the world, it may be that "no actual setups of kind $X$ will exist. The conditional statement is then called a counterfactual conditional". How can the conditional claim then be useful to us? Godfrey-Smith suggests that "part of the answer surely lies in the idea of approximation". Granted there are scenarios that approximate $X$ and $Y$, perhaps "we can go from 'If X, then Y' to 'If approximately X, then approximately Y.' This pattern of inference is not a deductively valid one; it can definitely go wrong. But sometimes, with the aid of other information, we might be able to make this move" (p. 308). In light of this, "a simplified scientific model can be seen as a device that generates a lot of conditionals, if-then statements, where most of these are counterfactuals, but some are usefully close to nature".

  • 13.5 Consensus

    • Clearly related to the topics of truth and belief is the issue of consensus in science. Note, "consensus is agreement ... that has been made explicit ... usually ... on some particular theory or approach" (p. 308). Furthermore, "there can be consensus without complete agreement and also without complete confidence". To some, such as Feyerabend, pressure to form consensus may be harmful as it may hold back "the creative exploration of ideas". It is better if scientists can come up with "new ideas and hang onto them, despite their problems, to see how they might develop. A situation of permanent diversity is something [Feyerabend] would welcome" (p. 308-9). Sometimes however, e.g. when decisions must be made "in a way guided by science", the consensus view, if it exists, is needed (e.g. by a government body). In turn, when there is "complete agreement within a scientific community ... things are easy", but when there is "a view endorsed by a majority, but with some dissenters ... things get more difficult". Note, appealing to some Bayesian approach for choosing actions via expectations does not immediately solve the issue, since dissenters can presumably advocate for different probability assignments. One thing we can do though, in the case of "wide disagreement" is to at least "work out what the really disastrous outcomes are ... and avoid them".

    Godfrey-Smith distinguishes between a spontaneous consensus, which occurs when a scientific community is asked a question, and it is quickly found that the relevant matter is settled and that there is a leading view, and a curated consensus, when "an agreement is reached", but only after there is "a more organized attempt to pull things together", to "work out whether lingering uncertainties are serious or not" and after a good amount of debate on what a public statement should look like. Yet, there are risks involved: debates may be closed off too early, leading to bad judgements and decisions, or debates may go on for too long, costing resources and opportunities. Also, in contemporary societies, there may be great pressure to share "the basis for ... policy being applied" (p. 310), and so the scientific community may have to "say something as definite as possible". In turn, it may be difficult to continue work on alternative views if a consensus has been publicly declared.

    Godfrey-Smith recalls his three levels of looking at science, and deems spontaneous consensus to be a level 2 matter. In contrast, he regards a curated consensus to be a level 3 affair, as there "can be feedback between the external consumers of scientific information and a scientific community". However, there could conceivably also be a scenario with little feedback, making it level 2. The rest of the section looks into the historical controversy involved in reaching the curated consensus on HIV being the cause of AIDS. On the one hand, an early statement in 1988, though correct, was arguably premature as most of the evidence at that point was observational. On the other hand, long after it became conclusive, a few scientists, such as Duesberg kept contesting the consensus, with catastrophic consequences for policy makers who followed their advice. This whole "case ... reminds us that there is a downside to the initially appealing idea that it's always good to be as open-minded as possible and avoid closing off debate" (p. 312), and highlights the challenge of balancing the risk of closing off debates too early versus letting contrarians "override a sensible use of hard-won knowledge" (p. 310), when decisions have to be made. Indeed, it "would [be] fine to keep questioning if you could have policy guided by the weight of expert opinion ... without that opinion being undermined by doubters. That is the best-case scenario. But this has become harder and harder to achieve in recent cases" (p. 313), e.g. with medicine or climate change.

  • 13.6 Occam's Razor

    • Occam's razor is sometimes expressed as do not postulate entities beyond necessity; more concretely it's the idea that all else being equal, we should prefer simpler explanations to more complex ones. But what (p. 314):

    exactly is the simplicity we are supposed to seek? First, fewer or more entities might be used in a theory. Alternatively, perhaps what matters is not fewer entities, but fewer kinds of things ... Third, we might prefer less complicated over more complicated causal arrangements. This can conflict with other kinds of simplicity; you might be able to tell a story with a small number of causal factors, but only if they are arranged in a very elaborate way. Fourth, a simpler theory might be more compact when written down, though this will depend on what language is chosen, and it's hard to see it as an important matter except as a guide to other kinds of simplicity.

    Later on in the section, Godfrey-Smith also notes that some "people say that the defining feature of a simpler hypothesis is the fact that it constrains observations more than other hypotheses - this is what simplicity is" (p. 317). However, Godfrey-Smith argues that this view seems to be mostly motivated by a desire to make testing simpler, and I would add that it need not align with the other senses of simplicity. On the other hand, we might prefer simpler theories if we believe "they are more likely to be true", i.e. due to epistemic reasons, as opposed to preferring them "because simple theories are easier to work with", i.e. due to methodological reasons. Indeed, practicality may be a big deal and for some, the razor "must be justified because it would be a disaster if we had to give it up. But that is not much of a justification" (p. 315). On a separate note, Godfrey-Smith says that the kind of simplification associated with modelling "is not an application of Occam's razor if we know that a simple model is inaccurate". I am not sure I fully agree on this point, as we do seem to appeal to something in the spirit of Occam's razor when we build models (e.g. assume that a relatively simple model can accurately represent a mechanism seen in some complex real world setting). However setting aside the issue of whether modelling (perhaps implicitly) involves Occam's razor, the "more important issue is whether it is justified to prefer simple theories because this is more likely to get us to the truth. If so, why should this be? Why should the world be simple?" There are faith-based or aesthetic appeals, which Godfrey-Smith rejects. Some argue that the history of science shows a trend towards simplicity. However, Godfrey-Smith rejects this too, and gives the example of the number of posited fundamental physical particles as something that has gone down and up repeatedly across history. Godfrey-Smith goes on to give his own view of when and why the razor is justified. Firstly, there are "areas in science where simplicity of a particular kind is preferable in hypotheses because of how we think that part of the world works" (p. 316). For example, "evolutionary hypotheses that require smaller numbers of favorable mutations are generally preferable ... [as] they require fewer unlikely events to happen". Next, he talks about the relationship between simplicity and testability. He notes that Popper thought "that simple theories, in many cases, can be easily falsified if they are false. So it is good to work with them; they take risks in the way he liked". We are told that Popper used the example that "straight-line" hypotheses need fewer data points to be refuted than quadratics. In his chapter notes, Godfrey-Smith elaborates that the class of quadratics is a larger family that includes all linear hypotheses; so perhaps on the basis of them forming a smaller subclass, it can be argued that straight-line hypotheses are simpler and should be preferred if we can obtain good fit. However, Godfrey-Smith seems to think that a more rigorous basis for judging simplicity is in terms of the intrinsic complexity of a hypothesis (e.g. straight lines are simpler shapes / require fewer parameters) as opposed to the basis of falsifiability or hypothesis class size (though it does seem that intrinsic simplicity and hypothesis class size / falsifiability can be deeply interlinked).

    In any case, Godfrey-Smith says for the sake of argument, "let's grant the main idea: simple theories sometimes take more risks, and that makes them easier to falsify if they are false". On the flip side, it follows via some Bayesian reasoning that such hypotheses "get a [bigger] boost when things do turn out the way they require" (p. 317). Yet, it is not always clear how plausible such hypotheses are in the first place (i.e. what their priors are), as just being "simple" may not make something likely: e.g. Godfrey-Smith gives an example using bats and caves to highlight a scenario where a hypothesis' simplicity is motivated via a rather unlikely background assumption. In the end, Godfrey-Smith seems to favour Occam's razor due to the methodological advantages which he thinks it offers, simply put (p. 318):

    If you start with a simple theory, one with only a few causal factors, then when observations come in that clash with your view, you might be able to make more sense of what is going on than you could if you were working with a very complex theory. So it can be reasonable to start out working with simple views even though you expect to get pushed toward more complex ones. The "pushing" works better - more clearly - when you do this.

    So the takeaway would be that, in "many situations it is good to start by working with simple theories, and see how they fare, because this is more likely to get us to the truth in the end" (p. 319).

  • My comments

    • I think it's reasonable to hld that there is a holism about meaning, and that this holism entails that the exact satisfaction conditions of any particular statement could only be specified by considering one's entire "web of beliefs". However applying such a holistic outlook may be difficult. In any case, I think that Ramsey's approach to truth is meant to serve as a theoretical exercise, since specifying explicit satisfaction conditions may also require "ground truth" access to what the actual possible states of the world are - a level of access we don't have. Now, if we try to avoid such issues about access to the external world by limiting ourselves to our perceptions, i.e. if we adopt some kind of phenomenalism, we will still face some of the same sort of issues that afflicted the Logical Positivists' verification principle. That is, issues related to: the holism of testing, background assumptions, and the underdetermination of statements by data (when statements are generalisations) when we try to verify the truth of a statement. Also, setting these long-standing issues relating to verification aside, even the task of reformulating a statement in terms of definite satisfaction conditions with respect to perceptions seems like a very challenging theoretical exercise, and if we really want to go down this road, we may ask ourselves if we are still really aiming for a theory of truth as opposed to a theory of meaning or something less ambitious. In the end, it seems to me that the approach to truth advocated by Ramsey and Godfrey-Smith in 13.3 is helpful as a theoretical framework, and perhaps we should just admit that the standard of truth it sets is challenging if not impossible to meet in practice.

    On the topic of Occam's razor, I would endorse it on methodological grounds for reasons similar to Godfrey-Smith's. Simpler theories should typically be easier to troubleshoot, adjust and expand. Ease of understanding and application benefit from opting for simpler explanations when there are both simple and complicated explanations that seem otherwise equally compelling. Now, I would not rule out that there might be deeper epistemic or metaphysical motivations for the razor, but I haven't come across anything convincing so far. In the theory of computation, I have heard some say that Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference can motivate the razor (i.e. provide a deeper basis for it), but from my reading of it, it in fact assumes the razor and does not in any sense "derive it". What it does show is that using the razor will always "get you to the truth" eventually, and the logarithm of the time it takes will be proportional with how complex the "truth" is. More generally, any other arguments that I have seen come out of computation theory in favour of the razor have seemed similarly methodological (e.g. it's much more feasible to systematically search small programs first to see whether they can generate a given pattern, before moving on to larger ones), and philosophically this isn't too surprising; why should truth necessarily be simple? "Spiritually", I'd like to think that there is something simple and fundamental at the core of reality from which the apparent complexity of the world emerges, but this is more of a wish than an argument.
