Chapter 7 — The Challenge from Sociology of Science

Summary goes here.

  • 7.1 Beyond Philosophy?

    • From the second half of the 20th century onwards, the sociology of science developed a great deal and "sometimes set itself up as a replacement, or 'successor discipline,' to the philosophy of science" (p. 152). Recalling the three level view Godfrey-Smith introduced in chapter 5, while some philosophers such as Kuhn went up to level 2, by looking at scientific communities as collectives, their treatment remained "rather self-contained and didn't have much to say about the third level. [However, the] sociology of science developed theories that explicitly cover the connections, including influences in both directions, between science and [broader] society as a whole."

  • 7.2 Robert Merton and the "Old" Sociology of Science

    • Robert Merton founded modern sociology. He was "interested in the ways that particular kinds of societies fostered or hindered modern science" (p. 153), but Godfrey-Smith focuses on his study of "the internal workings of scientific communities". Indeed, "in the 1940s Merton investigated science by giving a description of its norms... Merton said that the norms of science are universalism, communism, disinterestedness, and organized skepticism:

    Universalism is the idea that the personal attributes and social background of a person are irrelevant to the scientific value of the person's ideas. Communism involves the common ownership of scientific ideas and results. Anyone can make use of any scientific idea in his or her work; the French are not barred from using English results. The norm of disinterestedness is made questionable by some of Merton's own later ideas, but the idea is that scientists are supposed to act for the benefit of a common scientific enterprise rather than for personal gain. Organized skepticism is a community-wide pattern of challenging and testing ideas instead of taking them on trust.

    Later in the 1950s, Merton gave an account of the reward system in science:

    Merton claimed that the currency of scientific reward is recognition, especially recognition for being the first person to come up with an idea. This, Merton claimed, is the only property right recognized in science. Once an idea is published, it becomes common scientific property, according to the norm of communism. In the best case, a scientist is rewarded by having the idea named after him... Merton argued that the importance of recognition is made evident by the fact that the history of science is crammed with priority disputes.

    Godfrey-Smith acknowledges that in "the parts of science that have potential for commercialization, the nature of priority disputes has changed considerably" (p. 154), as rights to monetary rewards are at stake in addition to recognition. Still, the very fact that there are priority disputes when there is "no money at stake at all" supports Merton's claims. Merton further looked at what happens (p. 155):

    when the desire for reward overcomes everything else in a scientist's mind. The main 'deviant' behaviors that result are fraud, plagiarism, and libel and slander. Of these, Merton held that fraud is very rare, plagiarism somewhat less rare, and libel and slander very common. Fraud is rare, he said, largely because of a rigorous internal policing by scientists, which derives partly from their own ambitions but also from organized skepticism. (Fraud may have become more common since Merton's time; I'll return to this in a later chapter.) Plagiarism does happen, but the most usual outlet for deviant behavior is libel and slander of competitors. More precisely, what we find is a special form of slander that relates to the reward structure of science: accusation of plagiarism. This is vastly more common than actual theft.

    Moving on:

    Merton also has a poignant discussion of the fact that the kind of recognition that is the basic reward in science will be given to only a small number of scientists. There are not enough laws and constants for everyone to get one. The result is mild forms of deviancy such as the mania to publish. For pedestrian workers who cannot hope to produce a world-shaking discovery, publication becomes a substitute for real recognition.

    Godfrey-Smith disagrees with Merton's analysis here: there may be a mania to publish, but at least in recent times, it is more so due to university administrations and their utilisation of publication count as a performance metric. What's more, though most scientists will not be recognised for any ground-breaking work, they will "often have real standing in a small community of people who work on the same problems. [This recognition] even in a tiny community of colleagues can be a significant source of motivation" (p. 156).

    Finally, we may think of Merton's norms as constituting the "motor" of science, and recognition-based reward as the "fuel" that runs it, in an analogy to help us understand how these aspects of Merton's work relate. But this simple picture glosses over clear tensions. For example, "what remains of the norm of disinterestedness if we accept Merton's analysis of the reward system. What we seem to have is not disinterestedness, but a special kind of ambition and self-interest" (p. 156). Still Merton's "old" style of sociology of science seems to provide valuable insights about the "the rewards and incentives that motivate individual scientists, and we have an account of how these individual behaviors generate the higher-level social features of science" (p. 157) such as its norms.

  • 7.3 The Rise of the Strong Program

    • "Old"-school sociology focuses on explaining the social structure of science, that is its norms and institutions, while newer approaches have also tried to explain its content, i.e. (p. 157) "why scientists believe what they do, why they behave as they do, and how scientific thinking and practice change over time." Merton, in alignment with Logical Empiricism, viewed scientific "theories [as] networks of predictive generalizations ... shaped mainly by data", a perspective that did not directly bear on his sociological analysis of science. In contrast, some newer approaches, inspired by "Kuhn's ideas about scientific change, holism about testing, the theory-ladenness of observation, and related themes", have adopted relativist positions that also are central to their sociological analyses. The first such newer approach we review is (p. 158):

    the strong program in the sociology of scientific knowledge. This project was developed by an interdisciplinary group based in Edinburgh, Scotland, in the 1970s, headed (to some extent) by Barry Barnes and David Bloor. A central idea of the strong program is the 'symmetry principle.' This principle holds that all forms of belief and behavior should be approached using the same kinds of explanations. In particular, we should not give totally different kinds of explanations for beliefs that we think are true and beliefs that we think are false. Our present assessment of an idea should have no effect on how we explain its history and social role. Applied to science, the symmetry principle tells us that scientific beliefs are products of the same general kinds of forces as other kinds of belief. Scientists are not some special breed of pure, disinterested thinkers who receive private bulletins from Nature. People of all kinds live in communities that have socially established local norms for regulating belief - norms for supporting claims, for handling disagreement, for working out who will be listened to and who will be ignored.

    So in science, individuals simply follow "local norms that are human creations, maintained by social interaction" like in any other community, and "we should not give the Real World a special role in the explanation of scientific belief that it does not have in the explanation of other beliefs that pass local community norms" (p. 159). What's more, explaining why some theories have succeeded while others failed by reference to some alleged facts about nature could violate the symmetry principle, as this presumably amounts to a post-hoc application of our current beliefs, which are contingent and could change someday, to produce explanations in lieu of the real historical and sociological factors that led to the successes and failures.
    Godfrey-Smith is critical of this stance, and argues instead that retroactive arguments can be justified, as our beliefs about nature (e.g. DNA has 4 base pairs) also apply to the how it was like in the past. I agree with Godfrey-Smith, and think that the strong program's position is not tenable as one has to presuppose a radical relativism, i.e. presuppose that our current scientific beliefs have no real basis in nature, which I reject.

    Indeed, the symmetry principle could be reconciled with some notion of empiricism, yet the strong program instead opted to analyse "scientific theories in terms of their relations to their social and political context ... The aim was to explain some scientific beliefs in terms of the political 'interests' of scientists and their place within society" (p. 160). Their take-away was that "scientific ideas will 'reflect the interests' of a social group". Godfrey-Smith, critical of the vagueness of terms such as "reflects", rebuts that it certainly:

    can sometimes be shown that the popularity of a scientific idea benefits a social group. But is this benefit supposed to explain the popularity of the scientific idea or not? If so, is the explanation supposed to be a causal explanation ... or some other kind?

    The suggestions that social attitudes could determine scientific beliefs drew criticism even from Kuhn, who himself had advocated for a form of relativism, but still regarded matters pertaining to truth and belief as part of the "internal affairs" of science, and held that scientific decision-making must be insulated from broader societal forces.
    In contrast, by applying relativism to science as a whole, the strong program asserted that "science has no special authority that extends beyond [its] local norms ... We who live in science-dominated societies will find it compelling to say 'science really is the best way of learning about the world.' But ... saying [such] things is just expressing our local norms." (p. 161), and so by their account, science need not be any "truer" than any non-scientific belief system.
    Finally, the section ends with a brief reference to the "problem of reflexivity". This is the sociological version of the problem of what happens if we apply relativism to itself (p. 162):

    If all beliefs are to be explained in terms of the same kinds of social factors, and no set of local norms can be judged 'really' superior, from an external stand point, then what about the theories found in sociology of science? ... Mostly, the sociologists of science accepted that their claims were true of their own ideas. They accepted that their own theories were only justified according to local social norms.

    The admission of such a limitation, though respectable, does seem to leave the door open for questioning the strong program's validity, and indeed the problem of reflexivity "led to interminable discussions that weighed the field down".

  • 7.4 Leviathan, Latour, and the Manufacture of Facts

    • This section looks at two influential books. The first, Schafer and Shapin's Leviathan and the Air Pump (1985), presents similar positions to the strong program but has a historical focus. In particular, the book discusses the "rise of experimental science in seventeenth-century England" (p. 163) and focuses on a dispute between Boyle and Hobbes "over some specific scientific issues [regarding vacuums] and over the right form for scientific work to take". Given the background of violence in the 17th century Europe, both thinkers were concerned with how to handle disputes and achieve consensus.
    Though not elaborated upon by Godfrey-Smith, Hobbes argued that natural philosophy should stick to deduction and first-principles causal reasoning, deeming experimental work as being artificial and uncertain: i.e. a recipe for further disagreement. According to Hobbes, experimenters can be misled by their apparatuses and their results need interpretation in any case, and so experiments alone cannot settle disagreements, unlike irrefutable geometrical arguments and deductions, which can. Boyle, whose views prevailed and were embodied by the Royal Society of London that he helped found, countered that rigorous and well documented experiments in fact provide the best means for learning about nature, and for the settlement of disputes, granted that views about nature are framed in terms of testable claims.
    Here, Shapin and Schaffer argue that Boyle engaged in a sleight-of-hand, as his "strategy was to subtly replace [empirically indeterminate] questions with other questions that could be the topic of experimental work" (p. 164). Focusing on the debate about pure vacuums; Boyle's re-framing of the question of whether there can be empty space, into whether there can be a volume with arbitrarily little air, is presented as shifting goalposts, something that Hobbes also called out.

    Using Wittgenstein's terms, "Boyle's treatment of crucial terms like 'vacuum' established a new language game. This language game was a key component in a new form of life, the form of life of experimental science" (p. 165). A "'form of life' is something like a set of basic practices, behaviors, and values", and a language game refers to "a collection of habits in the use of language that contribute to a form of life and make sense within it." Importantly:

    Wittgenstein opposed a view of language in which words and sentences are attached to their own particular meanings (mental images, perhaps) that determine how language is used. Instead, Wittgenstein claimed we should think of the socially maintained patterns of language use as all there is to the "meaning" of language.

    With this, Shapin and Schaffer insinuate that reality/nature itself plays no essential or defining role in "the manufacture of facts" (p. 166), i.e. in the results produced by experimental science. Godfrey-Smith contends that while it is important to appreciate "that facts in general are made rather than found", and to "reject a picture of the scientist as a passive receiver of information from the world", we should not tend to the other extreme and deny an important role for reality in the "manufacture of facts", e.g. by erroneously claiming, as Shapin and Schaffer do, that "it is ourselves and not reality that is responsible for what we know".

    The section moves on to Latour and Woolgar's laboratory life (1979), which is an account of a Nobel prize winning lab's work on the regulation of human growth hormone. However (p. 166-7):

    in their account, [Latour and Woolgar] ignored most of what a normal description of a piece of science would focus on. They ignored the state of our knowledge of hormones; they ignored the ways in which experimental methods in the field are able to discriminate alternative chemical structures ... Instead, Latour saw himself as an anthropologist visiting an unfamiliar tribe and trying to work out what meaning their strange behaviors had. Latour and Woolgar described the lab as a complicated device where chemicals, small animals, and reams of blank paper went in at one end, and small printed pieces of paper-journal articles and technical reports-came out at the other end. In between the two, a huge amount of "processing" went on ... aimed at taking scientific claims and building structures of "support" around them, so they would eventually be taken as facts. A key step in this process is hiding the human work involved ... to make it look as though it is not a human product but is given directly by nature.

    What many appreciated, besides Latour's witty and literary style, is that (p. 168):

    in Latour's approach, the sociologist studies the details of the internal dynamics of scientific work, especially disputes and negotiation about what has been established. The sociologist does not begin the story by taking for granted "pressures" or "interests" in society at large, and "nature" or the real world is not taken for granted either. Instead, both society and nature are seen as products, not causes, of the settlement of scientific controversies (Latour 1987). Where traditional empiricist philosophy had seen science as 'data-driven', and the strong program had seen science as 'interest-driven,'' Latour sees scientific work itself as the driver.

    Indeed, this perspective on scientific work "prompted a shift within the field. The strong program came to seem crude", but we must still remember that in:

    Latour's view, when we explain why one side succeeded and another failed in a scientific controversy, we should never give the explanation in terms of nature itself. Both sides will be claiming that they are the ones in tune with the facts. But when one side wins, that side's version of "the facts" becomes immune to challenge. Latour describes this final step as a process in which facts are created, or constructed, by scientific work ... This work includes hiding the human choices involved; to turn something into a fact is to make it look like it was just "given" by nature.

    Godfrey-Smith acknowledges the value of analysing how "the concepts of 'truth' and 'nature' ... are used as resources in arguments and discussion" (p. 169), but he criticises Latour's unwillingness to distinguish beliefs from reality itself, and his dismissive view towards the role of reality in the "manufacture" of scientific beliefs, though Godfrey-Smith also acknowledges that Latour has hardly been the only one with such attitudes. Godfrey-Smith partly attributes the emergence of such attitudes to a collapse in our confidence for the traditional picture for belief formation: "with objects affecting our minds through perception, leading to beliefs about the objects" (p. 170). This perceived collapse "has led to an exploration of all sorts of inversions and reversals of the usual relationships", e.g. instead of "there being a path from objects through perception to beliefs ... perhaps the path runs the other way; perhaps the objects that make up the world are a consequence of what we think and say." I agree with Godfrey-Smith that these inversions are not any better than the old picture and deeply appreciate the alternative he presents to Latour's view at the end of the section:

    scientists are not passive recipients of information about [hormones]; scientific life is full of wrangling, negotiation, and trade-offs, full of decisions about who to trust and how to persuade. These, Latour and Woolgar insist, are all choices. Yes, they are, but one thing scientists can choose to do is to have what they say guided by how experiments ... turn out. They can decide: if I see X, I will think and do this; if I see Y, I will think and do that. They might make this choice and then later change their mind or fudge things. But they also might not. If they do make decisions of this kind and stick to them, the world inside the mice then plays some role in constraining what scientists say. Whether we learn much about the world outside the San Diego mice - for example, other animals - is a further question. Questions about extrapolation and induction will not stay away for long. But this is a start.

  • My comments

    • Analysing the norms and reward systems found in science as Merton did (a "level 2" analysis), surely is valuable for determining how the enterprise can be improved and how sociological and interpersonal factors could be influencing scientific research and results. In this regard, even level 3 analyses similar to the strong program could be helpful, especially in our day and age (2026) as the global order and old institutions are weakening and changing (e.g. consider the increasing role of big tech in basic scientific research and its funding).
    However, it seems false to assume, as Bloor and Barnes, Schafer and Shapin, Latour and Woolgar, and others apparently did, that sociological and psychological forces chiefly determine scientific beliefs, and to relegate nature itself by denying it any major influence. On this point, only Merton and Godfrey-Smith seem reasonable by acknowledging nature's role in this chapter.
    Indeed, I would say that both Boyle and Hobbes appealed to processes beyond our feelings and attitudes, be it careful and well-documented experimentation, or deductive and mathematical reasoning (which I also take to be "objective"), as resources that drive the progress of scientific ideas. Indeed, I think that Boyle's experimentally driven approach, which involves the careful framing of nature's mysteries (such as the possibility of vacuums) into testable claims, combined with a commitment towards philosophical transparency and logical and mathematical rigour, while trying to maintain a self-critical attitude towards the sociological and cultural forces that may be shaping our thinking, presents a productive framework for improving our understanding of the natural world.
