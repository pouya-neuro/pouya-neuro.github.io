Chapter 1 — Marks of the Mental

  • 1.1 Aspects of Mentality

    • This section characterises the mind in terms of its faculties/capacities: perception, control of action, thought (which includes belief formation, simulating the world and planning). Also distinguished are the faculties of emotion and imagination (which seems close to thought), as well as memory (which may be regarded as the "perception of the past" [p. 7]).
    Next, Bayne distinguishes between "mental events, mental processes and mental states". Mental events are "dated, particular, happenings" such as given instances of specific sensations or thoughts. In contrast, mental processes such as reasoning or deliberation are said to "unfold over time and often involve transitions between mental states". Given the way that Bayne puts it, it seems to me that any mental process can be characterised as a temporally ordered sequence of mental events which are linked to each other by causal mechanisms in the mind.
    Bayne also notes, some philosophers reserve the term "mental state" exclusively for what Bayne calls standing states: "mental phenomena that do not 'unfold over time' in the way in which mental processes do. Beliefs are standing states". However, Bayne says that he will use the term "mental states" to refer to standing states, as well as mental processes and events.
    Bayne also discusses mental properties, which are like abstractions of mental states, in that many "different objects can have (or instantiate) the same property" whereas "mental states, events, and processes are what philosophers call particulars: they are datable and locatable occurrences". Bayne points out that there is a debate in philosophy as to whether mental states or mental properties are more primitive. Bayne himself prefers the position that "mental properties are more fundamental ... a mental state can be identified with a particular creature having ... a mental property at a time" (p. 8). In any case, the distinction between mental properties and states is not relevant to many topics, let alone the debate as to which is more basic.
    Finally, Bayne posits, "we have discussed the notion of mental faculties, events, processes, states, and properties, but what of minds themselves? What is the mind?" Perhaps, as opposed to reifying the mind and thinking of it as an object, we should regard the term as "a convenient shorthand for picking out the kinds of mental phenomena that we have surveyed here". Still, Bayne goes on to examine whether mental phenomena share any unifying properties by virtue of which they are bound to particular minds and distinguished from the non-mental.

  • 1.2 The privacy of the mental

    • "The attempt to find a defining feature of mentality is often referred to as the search for the ‘mark of the mental’" (p. 8). This feature could be a collection of properties that are jointly necessary and sufficient for mentality. The mind's privacy is sometimes deemed as such a feature. Intuitively, only you have direct and unmediated access to your own headache, unlike others who may only learn about it from your behaviour. Note that such an asymmetry of access does not characterize your non-mental physical states such as your height or weight. However, in the section's first box, Bayne questions this notion of asymmetric access by citing fMRI studies where the private thoughts of subjects are decoded, though with limited accuracy till now. Could there be fundamental limits to the access others can have to your mental states?

    In any case, Bayne holds that privacy can not serve as the mark of the mental for at least three further reasons. First, there are conditions where people seem to have worse access to their own mental states than others do, as seen in cases of Anton's syndrome (characterised by blindness that is denied). Such cases put "pressure on the intuitive idea that we always know our own states of mind better than other people do" (p. 10). Secondly, though one may have exclusive and direct access to certain mental states (e.g. one's itches), perhaps certain mental states, such as depression "might be more apparent to others than ... to oneself". "Third, there are mental states to which we lack any kind of first-person access". Bayne argues that sub-personal representational states are an example of such, which unlike our personal-level states (such as beliefs or visual experiences) typically pertain to low-level cognitive processing in brain that we cannot direct access and instead may only learn about through scientific enquiry. Bayne elaborates on the personal/subpersonal distinction in a box, noting how the former have been defined either as the mental states we can be conscious of, or as those states that may be used in reason-giving explanations (presumably for our behaviours), unlike subpersonal states, which are, again about 'brains and events in the nervous system', and which presumably can't independently suffice for the same kinds of explanations.
    Finally, the section ends with the important point that privacy, even if it didn't fail in being a mark of the mental, still "wouldn't provide us with a positive characterisation of mentality" (p. 11). In particular, privacy does not do enough to illuminate the nature of mentality: e.g. detailing who has access to a particular phenomenon does not detail the phenomenon itself.

  • 1.3 Intentionality

    • Intentionality is presented as a positive candidate for the mark of the mental. Brentano's thesis is that "all and only mental states are intentional" (p. 11) in that they are always "directed towards" / "about" objects or propositional contents. Often, mental states, e.g. the belief that "person $X$ was the first $Y$" can be parsed both in terms of their objects (person $X$) and in terms of its contents (the proposition that "$X$ was the first $Y$"). However, the section's only box considers cases where only one of the two senses of "aboutness" seems admissible. Before getting to the box, Bayne highlights "that the nature of intentional objects raises questions that are not easy to answer" (p. 13) and presents, as an example, the dilemma of specifying the object of a veridical visual experience of a spider and of an identical hallucinatory experience. If intentional objects are to be solely determined by the contents of experience, then these two experiences should have the same object. Yet, the hallucination cannot be about a real spider, for there is no such object, while the veridical visual experience should be about a real spider, because that real spider seems to be the best candidate for what the veridical experience is about. Like with hallucinations, it can seem difficult to say what the objects of thoughts about fictional entities could be.

    In any case, the objects of intentional states are "only part of the story. Another part of the story concerns the ways in which intentional states can be evaluated". Different intentional "modes" or "attitudes" such as beliefs and desires relate to their objects in different ways (beliefs have truth conditions, whereas desires have satisfaction conditions). "A full account of intentionality would need to say how many intentional attitudes there are, and how they are related to each other". In a box, Bayne notes that since many intentional states "have propositions as their contents, intentional attitudes are often referred to as propositional attitudes", but he goes on to critique this equation by presenting intentional states that do not seem to be propositional: loving someone or fearing a monster aren't about propositions, rather they are emotional states directed at objects.

    Bayne then challenges the notion that only mental states are intentional by noting that language and other representational devices (like maps) are directed at things too. However, these counterexamples appear to have derived intentionality, in that their directedness is the result of human convention, whereas mental states have underived intentionality (e.g. a perception doesn't need to be first interpreted in order to be about something). So is underived intentionality the mark of the mental? Are there mental states that are not intentional? Bayne presents, as contended counterexamples, visual afterimages and moods. Perhaps afterimages are about illusory physical objects or phenomena, and moods are directed at the whole world or at one's self. In any case, Bayne's takeaway is that the mental status of afterimages or moods does not seem to depend on their intentional status, and this crucially suggests "that there is more to our conception of the mental than intentionality" (p. 15).

  • 1.4 Consciousness

    • We may assign consciousness to organisms or to particular mental states. However, in Bayne's view, an organism can only be "conscious in virtue of having conscious mental states" (p. 16) and there is no distinct organism-level consciousness property. Bayne notes that conscious states are often distinguished by appealing to what they are about, i.e. their intentional objects/contents. Bayne also notes that the term consciousness itself has multiple senses and that it's often mixed up with related terms such as "experience" or "awareness", and so he hones in on the term "phenomenal consciousness": there "is ‘something that it is like’ for a creature to be in a phenomenally conscious state, and phenomenal states differ from each other in terms of what it’s like to be in them ... [i.e.] in terms of their phenomenal character." (p. 16-7). The characters of phenomenal states are also picked out by the term qualia, and as the section's box details, qualia can be highly controversial. In an uncontentious sense, qualia simply are the appearances of conscious mental states, but qualia can also be contentiously associated with features such as being (p. 17):

    non-intentional (i.e., not explainable in terms of intentional content); intrinsic (i.e., not definable in terms of their relations to other states); ineffable (i.e., not able to be communicated); and private (i.e., not able to be compared across individuals). The claim that there are sensory appearances that have one or more of these features has been the subject of a longstanding dispute between ‘qualiaphiles’ (who hold that contentious qualia of some kind exist) and ‘qualiaphobes’ (who hold that only uncontentious qualia exist).

    Irrespective of such debates, Bayne questions whether consciousness can be the mark of the mental. It is commonly viewed that only mental states can be conscious, but are all mental states conscious? For example, standing states like beliefs are mental states that don't cease during dreamless sleep. In response, some such as Searle have advocated the connection principle: "it is of the essence of mentality that a mental state must be accessible to consciousness" (p. 18). Bayne, however, doesn't believe that this restrictive view is merited. He points to "subpersonal states that are not accessible to consciousness, even in principle", which are nonetheless routinely regarded as mental states in cognitive neuroscience as they are constitutive of cognition. Some personal-level states like character traits (e.g. shyness or conscientiousness) also seem to lie outside of the scope of the connection principle. Bayne clarifies, one can "become conscious that one is shy, but that doesn’t mean that shyness is itself a conscious state" (p. 18). Therefore Bayne rejects consciousness as the mark of the mental.

  • 1.5 Folk psychology

    • "Folk psychology" refers to the intuitive conception of the mind that most people have, indeed "explaining, understanding, and predicting what someone might do by invoking their perceptions, beliefs, intentions, emotions, and so on is second nature to us" (p. 18). However, "folk psychology raises a great number of questions" (p. 19):

    • "One question concerns its content. What exactly is the folk-psychological conception of the mind?" Do mental states cause actions or only rationalize them. Is thought fundamentally language-like? How does introspection work? Answering such questions may require a "science of" folk psychology.
    • "A second question posed by folk psychology concerns its acquisition" (p. 20). It seemingly does not require formal teaching as even uneducated people have it. Perhaps we acquire by "intuitive theorizing, building up a model of human behaviour in the way in which a scientist might", or implicitly by "learning the meaning of psychological terms – terms such as ‘want’, ‘think’, ‘know’ and ‘see’? Or is folk psychology part of our innate endowment?" Or maybe all these factors play a role.
    • "A third question concerns the application of folk-psychological concepts". On what grounds, and by what methods do we apply folk-psychological concepts to others and to ourselves, and can these generate knowledge?
    • "A fourth question concerns the accuracy of folk psychology. To what degree is our intuitive conception of the mind correct ... some theorists have argued that we should take seriously the possibility that the very categories of folk psychology fail to refer to genuine features of reality. This view is known as eliminativism".
    • On a similar note, what is "the relationship between folk psychology and our understanding of the natural world in general. Folk psychology makes reference to beliefs, desires, decisions, pains, and itches, but what exactly are these things? Are they physical phenomena?" Basically, we need an account of how folk-psychology lines up against the physical and cognitive sciences. Or they at odds or can they correspond?
    Finally, in a box, Bayne remarks on how there can be differences in intercultural differences in folk-psychology, giving the example of the "fundamental attribution error", which refers to the tendency to focus on "trait-based explanations of behaviour and ignore situational explanations" (p. 19). Research eventually showed that this "error" is peculiar to individualist societies and not prevalent in more collectivist and conformist societies. Still, intercultural similarities seem much greater than such differences, and it seems that "all human societies explain human behaviour by appeal to desires, thoughts, bodily sensations, intentions, beliefs, and emotions" (p. 19).
  • 1.6 Conclusion

    • Bayne believes there may be no single mark of the mental. Mentality could be a cluster concept. In any case, intentionality and consciousness are properties of the mind that stand out. What are their places in nature, can "they be accounted for in the same general kinds of ways as (say) digestion, respiration, and photosynthesis, or do they resist ordinary forms of scientific analysis?" (p. 21). Also, how do the two concepts relate? The most common view used to be that they are fundamentally distinct: aside from a few cases (like emotions) that were both (p. 21):

    it was widely assumed that mental states could be sorted into two, relatively distinct, classes: those that were intentional (but not phenomenal), and those that were phenomenal (but not intentional). Beliefs, desires and other propositional attitudes were placed in the first category, while perceptual experiences and bodily sensations were placed in the second category.

    This is the so-called separatist conception of mentality, and since the start of 21st century, it has been superseded by the opposite position of inseparatism (p. 21-22):

    intentionality and (phenomenal) consciousness are deeply intertwined and cannot be understood in isolation from each other. Some inseparatists embrace an ‘intentionality-first’ approach to the mind, arguing that we need to appeal to intentionality in order to understand consciousness. Other inseparatists embrace a ‘consciousness-first’ approach, arguing that we must appeal to consciousness in order to understand intentionality.

  • My comments

    • At the time of writing, I find myself to be a fairly radical deflationist, if not an outright eliminativist, about most facets of the mind. The mind, its faculties and most mental states seem like folk psychological concepts that owe their "being" to the utility that they afford us in describing the "going-ons" of our nervous systems. To be clear, I would not deny that perception refers to something real, but that the "something" is nothing over and beyond the activity of the physical mechanisms of the nervous system by which an organism tracks and represents the state of their external environment. Likewise, privacy and intentionality do not strike me as elusive properties of "mental states" that could not be well accounted for by neural mechanisms and physical states. As such, I would also do away with the search for a "mark of the mental": the nervous system and its physical processes are already distinguishable enough as entities and occurrents. However, there is one aspect of mentality I would strongly oppose the same eliminativist treatment for, and that is consciousness. My first-person access to it convinces me of the reality of it, and phenomenal experiences do not appear to be reducible to convenient socially constructed concepts for picking out certain kinds of physical states in the brain. To be clear, I am not pessimistic about science: I believe consciousness science can develop and be well integrated with other scientific domains, it seems, after all, like a very natural fact of life and of the world. It may however, take a lot of strides and conceptual advances till we get there, because there isn't to my understanding anything in the current natural sciences that seems to clearly entail or even anticipate consciousness; so at present, it does present an anomaly. Perhaps it is generic and ubiquitous across the universe, perhaps it is special and rare; in any case the real search, it seems to me, is for the physical "mark of the conscious".
